# Develop

The right panel holds the modules in pipeline order. All controls are live at full quality. Double-click a slider to reset it. ⌘Z and ⇧⌘Z undo and redo; `\` shows the unedited image. The Develop menu holds Auto Adjust, the crop, spot removal, red-eye, sensor dust and touch-up tools, the White Balance Selector, new masks and brush size, and View › Show Clipping shows clipping on the image; the Edit menu holds undo, redo and Copy and Paste Settings.

**Typed values.** Most sliders show their value as a number you can click: type a value and press Return or Tab (or click elsewhere) to apply it, or Escape to cancel. If the value changes while the field is open, because the slider moved or another image opened, the field follows it and anything typed is dropped. ↑ and ↓ step it by the last digit shown, ⇧↑ and ⇧↓ by ten steps. The unit can be typed or left out, and the value is clamped to the slider's range. A typed value is an ordinary edit and lands in history like a drag. Temperature takes a typed value in Kelvin. The four tone ranges, HSL, the B&W Mix, the luminance sliders under the three small colour wheels and export quality don't take typed values yet.

## Modules

**White Balance.** Temperature and tint, starting from the camera's own setting. As Shot returns to it. Click the Kelvin number beside Temperature to type one (2000–12000 K).

The menu above the sliders offers Lightroom's starting points: **As Shot** (the camera's setting), **Auto** (an estimate from the picture, the same one Auto Adjust makes, without touching exposure or contrast), and the light sources **Daylight** (5500 K), **Cloudy** (6500 K), **Shade** (7500 K), **Tungsten** (2850 K), **Fluorescent** (3800 K, leaning a little magenta to cancel the green of the tubes) and **Flash** (5500 K). Moving a slider afterwards makes the setting **Custom**. Latent's tint scale is its own, so the numbers don't match Lightroom's. When the raw's maker notes record the camera's own presets, a **Camera presets** section follows: the camera's Daylight, Shade, Cloudy, Tungsten, Fluorescent, Flash and Kelvin settings, as that camera defines them, turned into temperature and tint the same way As Shot is. Files without them (many cameras, and every JPEG or TIFF) show only the generic list.

**White Balance Selector.** Click the eyedropper, or press W, then click something in the photo that ought to be neutral: a grey card, a white wall, a cloud in shade. Temperature and tint change to make that spot grey, and the selector puts itself away. It reads a small patch of the raw data under the click, so it ignores the tone curve and every colour adjustment. A spot that is clipped, or too dark to have a reliable colour, is refused with a message in the status bar and the selector stays on for another try. Esc leaves it.

**Profile.** The row above White Balance sets the photo's starting colour and contrast, before any slider. Click the profile's name for the menu:

- **Latent Standard** is Latent's own rendering from the camera's colour matrix, the look every photo had before profiles existed. A photo that never had a profile chosen uses it, and always will.
- **Latent Neutral** has a little less contrast and gentler colour, a quiet starting point that leaves room to edit. **Latent Flat** goes further: very low contrast and muted colour, holding the most shadow and highlight detail, for grading.
- **Creative**: **Vivid** (more saturation and contrast, strongest on muted colours and a little easier on skin), **Portrait** (softer contrast, skin tones drawn together and a touch lighter and less red) and **Landscape** (richer foliage, deeper skies, a little more contrast).
- **Black & White**: **Monochrome** turns each colour into its brightness. The **Yellow**, **Red** and **Green Filter** versions work like a coloured filter on black and white film: yellow darkens blue skies a little, red darkens them a lot and pales skin, green lightens foliage and darkens reds and lips.
- **Camera Profiles for** your camera lists the DNG camera profiles (.dcp files) you have imported for it, and **Other Cameras** the rest, each with the camera it was made for. A profile made for another camera still works, but its colour is meant for that camera's sensor.

Creative and black and white profiles have an **Amount** slider, 0 to 200% (black and white to 100%): 0 is Latent Standard, 100% the look as designed, and above that the look is pushed further. Basic profiles and camera profiles have no Amount. Choosing a profile is an edit like any other: it lands in history, ⌘Z undoes it, and `\` shows the photo without it. Your sliders stay where they are when you switch, and work on top of the profile.

**Camera profiles (DCP).** A .dcp file describes how one camera model sees colour. You can get them from Adobe's free DNG Converter (which installs Adobe's profiles on your Mac), from some camera makers, or make your own from a colour chart with a tool such as dcamprof. Latent includes none and never downloads any. To add them, choose **Develop › Import Camera Profile…**, or **Import Profile…** at the bottom of the Profile menu, and pick one or more .dcp files; Latent checks that each is a readable profile and keeps a copy in its own profiles folder in Application Support, so the original can move or go. Importing the same file twice does nothing; a different file with the same name is kept as "Name 2".

Latent reads what makes a profile's colour: its colour matrices and forward matrices for one or two calibration lights, mixed for your white balance the way the DNG specification describes; its hue, saturation and value tables (including three-dimensional ones); its look table; and its exposure offset. With a camera profile chosen, its matrices replace the camera matrix Latent otherwise uses. White Balance still sets the camera's multipliers the way it always does, so a temperature can read a few kelvin differently from Adobe's for the same light.

A profile's own **tone curve** is off to start with: Latent keeps its own tone mapping (the sigmoid behind Contrast and Mid Grey, which holds highlights well past white) and takes only the profile's colour. A profile that carries a curve shows **Use profile's tone curve**; tick it to apply the curve on top of Latent's tone mapping, which is closer to the camera maker's contrast but usually stronger than in other editors, because those use the curve *instead* of a curve of their own.

A photo whose edit names a camera profile this Mac doesn't have (edited on another Mac, say) shows in Latent Standard, and the Profile row says which profile is missing. Import that profile and the photo looks as it was made. Copy and paste, presets and snapshots carry the profile as the **Profile** group.

**Tone.** Exposure in stops, contrast and mid-grey for the sigmoid tone curve. **Highlights, Shadows, Whites and Blacks** each brighten or darken one band of tones without moving mid grey. They change exposure per pixel by its brightness, so colours keep their hue, there are no halos, and tones never swap order. **Auto** (⌘U) sets exposure, contrast and white balance from the image itself, backing exposure off if it would blow the brightest highlights, then Highlights, Shadows, Whites, Blacks, Vibrance and Saturation on top: Whites and Blacks put the brightest and darkest few pixels just short of white and black, Highlights and Shadows pull in a scene with a wide range (a flat one is left alone), and Vibrance and Saturation lift a muted picture a little and leave a colourful one as it is. It is one step in history. **Shift-double-click** Highlights, Shadows, Whites or Blacks to set just that slider the Auto way, leaving the rest as they are; a plain double-click still resets it. On an HDR screen, **HDR display** shows highlights above paper white using the screen's headroom.

**Exposure eyedropper and Black Offset.** The eyedropper beside Exposure sets Exposure from the photo: click it, then click (or drag a rectangle over) something that should be middle grey, and Exposure changes so that area's light, measured before Exposure, lands on 18% grey. It is one step in history, Esc puts it away, and it stays within Exposure's −5 to +5. Option-click the eyedropper to choose another target: **50% of the display** (the edit's Mid Grey, which the tone curve shows at half brightness) or a **Custom** scene level. **Advanced**, beside it, holds **Black Offset**, darktable's black level correction: a small amount subtracted everywhere in linear light right after Exposure, above zero to deepen the blacks, below zero to lift them. It doesn't replace Blacks, which still works on top.

**Presence.** Texture (fine local contrast, 1–4 px), Clarity (mid-scale, midtone-weighted), Dehaze (dark-channel prior in linear light; negative adds haze), Vibrance (saturation that favours muted colours and spares skin) and **Saturation**, which moves every colour by the same proportion: −1 takes all the colour out, +1 doubles it.

**Crop and Straighten.** R opens the tool: drag inside to move, edges and corners to resize, with aspect presets that follow the image orientation (1:1, 3:2, 4:3, 5:4, 5:7, 16:9 and their tall forms, the original's own, or **Custom…**, which takes any width and height, such as 1.85 × 1). Straighten runs −45° to 45° and the crop shrinks to avoid empty corners, growing back as the angle returns. **Auto** levels the picture by the straight lines in it: horizons, rooflines, door frames, the edges of a building. To straighten by a line of your own, hold ⌘ and drag along something that should be level or upright with the crop tool on (or click the ruler button, then drag): when you let go, the picture turns so that line is level, or plumb if it was nearer upright. The crop is a sampling map, not a pipeline stage, so it costs no re-render. **Develop › Straighten** turns the picture by a tenth of a degree (⌥[ and ⌥]) or a whole degree (⌥⇧[ and ⌥⇧]) either way, each press its own undo step, with or without the crop tool on. **Reset** removes the crop and straighten; the Transform panel has its own Reset. See [Crop guides and the surround](#crop-guides-and-the-surround) and [Transform and Upright](#transform-and-upright) below.

**Spot Removal.** H arms the tool. Click a spot to place a patch with an automatic source; drag from the click to choose the source; drag either circle to move it; ⌫ deletes the selected patch. [ and ] make the next patch, and the selected one, smaller or larger. **Heal** copies the source's texture and takes tone and colour from what surrounds the target on every side, so a gradient or an edge running through the patch carries on. **Clone** copies exactly. Patches apply in order, each seeing the ones before, so a patch next to an earlier one matches it. Up to 32 patches.

**Shape** switches between **Spot** and **Brush**. With Brush, drag along a wire, a hair or a dust streak: it becomes one patch when you let go, with its source placed beside it, across the stroke. Drag the dashed outline or its dot to move the source, or the solid outline to move the stroke. A click with Brush makes an ordinary circle. [ and ] resize the selected patch. A stroke counts as one of the 32 patches, however long, and keeps up to 256 points; a longer scribble is simplified to fit. Healing copies the source's texture, so pick a source without edges running along the stroke: a cloud edge or a horizon under the source shows up as a line.

**Remove** is the third mode, beside Heal and Clone, for taking something out of the picture with no source to pick; **Detect Objects** makes it fill just the object you brushed over. See [Remove and Detect Objects](#remove-and-detect-objects) below.

**Remove tool.** Remove, Heal and Clone are the three modes of one tool, the **Remove Tool** button (H), as in Lightroom. **Opacity** (0 to 100%) sets how much a patch replaces what is under it, in every mode: the selected patch's, every selected patch's, and the next one's; a patch below 100% shows its opacity in the pin list. **Tool Overlay** chooses when the patches are drawn: **Always** (the default), **Auto** (only while the pointer is over the photo), **Selected** (only the selected patches) or **Never**; hidden patches still apply and can still be clicked, and the choice is kept between launches. **Visualise Spots** sits beside it, as under Sensor Dust, where its Contrast slider is.

The pin list under the sliders has a row for each patch, in the order they apply: its number, mode, shape (Spot, Brush, or Object once Detect Objects has found one) and any opacity below 100%. Click a row to select that patch, ⌘- or ⇧-click to select several, × to delete one. **Select All** selects every patch, so Opacity or ⌫ acts on them all; **Delete All** removes them. On the photo, **Option-drag** draws a marquee and deletes every patch whose pin (its target's centre, a stroke's start) is inside it when you let go; **Option-click** deletes the patch under the pointer. A Heal or Clone patch's source is drawn dashed, joined to its target by a line.

**Red-Eye**, under Spot Removal. Y arms the tool. Click a red pupil to add a spot, and drag while adding to size it; drag inside a spot to move it and its rim to resize it. ⌫ removes the selected spot, and [ and ] resize it. **Pupil Size** sets the selected spot's size (and the next one's), and **Darken** its strength. **Auto** finds faces with Apple's Vision framework on this Mac, with nothing sent anywhere, and adds a spot for each pupil that is actually red; brown and dark eyes are left alone. Only flash-red pixels inside a circle change, so a generous circle is harmless. Spots follow crop, straighten and rotation. Up to 32 spots.

**Pet Eye**, the Red-Eye tool's second mode, chosen with the **Red Eye | Pet Eye** switch above Pupil Size. An animal's eye glows green, yellow or white in a flash (light thrown back by the tapetum behind its retina), so no colour test can find it and Auto doesn't try. Choose Pet Eye, then draw the circle over the glowing pupil itself: everything inside it becomes a dark pupil that keeps a trace of its shading, with a soft edge so the iris around it is untouched. **Pupil Size** is the circle, and **Darken** the strength. **Add Catchlight** puts a small round highlight in the pupil, up and to the left as you look at the photo, so the eye doesn't look flat; it is on for new spots and can be switched for the selected one. Switching the selected spot between Red Eye and Pet Eye changes how it is corrected. Pet Eye spots count among the 32, follow crop and rotation, and travel with red-eye spots.

**Highlight Reconstruction.** Recovery and threshold pull clipped channels back toward neutral before the colour matrix. This repairs clipped colour; it doesn't move tones, which is what Highlights and Whites do. The **Method** menu chooses how: **Blend (neutral)**, what Latent has always done, or **Inpaint Opposed (keeps colour)**, after darktable's method of the same name. Inpaint Opposed rebuilds each clipped channel from the two channels opposite it, scaled by the colour of the unclipped pixels around the photo's clipped areas, so a blown sky stays a little blue and blown skin a little warm instead of turning white. It never darkens a channel. Recovery sets how much of the rebuilt colour is used and Threshold where a channel counts as clipped. The colour is measured once over the whole frame, so the preview, 100% and an export agree. It works on raws; merged DNGs keep the blend.

**Sensor Dust**, under Red-Eye. Dust on the sensor shows as faint round shadows in plain areas, the same spots in every photo, clearest at small apertures (f/11 and beyond). **Find Spots** looks for them on this Mac and heals each one, replacing whatever the last Find Spots found; the status bar says "Found 37 dust spots", and the count line under the controls says how many there are. **Sensitivity** (0–100, 50 to start) trades fainter and less round spots against false ones, and **Spot Size** (Small, Medium or Large: shadows 4–8, 6–16 or 12–40 pixels across on the sensor) says what to look for; the band is narrowed to what the photo's aperture and sensor predict, and moving either re-detects at once while the tool is on. **Spots…** (Develop › Sensor Dust) arms the tool and shows each spot as a thin ring: click a ring to remove a false spot, click the image to add one, ⌫ deletes the selected ring. **Clear** removes them all. **Visualise Spots** shows the photo as a high-pass view in which dust shadows stand out, on screen only, never in an export; **Contrast** sets how faint a shadow still shows. The spots are heal patches, healed ahead of your own patches, so a spot you also patched by hand takes the hand patch on top; Find Spots skips spots already under one. Before (`\`) shows the photo without them. Up to 200 spots per photo. **Save as dust map…** keeps the current spots as a map for this camera ("Nikon D750 · 12 Sep 2026 · 41 spots"), and **From dust map…** looks for a saved map's spots in this photo and heals the ones that are there; a map from another camera, or another sensor size, is refused. To remove dust from many photos at once, or to make a map from a reference photo, use Photo › Remove Dust… (see [Library](Library#remove-dust)). Auto Adjust (⌘U) never runs Find Spots.

**Touch-up**, under Sensor Dust, retouches faces: **Find Faces** finds them on this Mac with Apple's Vision framework, nothing sent anywhere, and lists each with a thumbnail and a switch ("Face 1", numbered left to right) so a face can be left alone; faces narrower than 64 pixels are counted "too small to retouch". **Skin Smoothing** softens the skin's fine texture and keeps its shape; **Teeth Whitening** takes the yellow out of the teeth; **Brighten Eyes** lifts the whites and the irises. All three run 0–100 and do nothing until a face is found and on. The skin, teeth and eye regions come from the face's landmarks and a skin-colour test, so brows, lips, eyes and nostrils are left out of the smoothing; **Show Skin Mask** tints the skin the sliders work on. **Remove blemishes** heals the spots **Find Blemishes** finds on the enabled faces' skin: dark or reddish round marks, up to 64 per photo, listed with a count and a mini Clear; switch it off to keep the list without healing. **Touch-up…** (Develop › Touch-up) arms the tool, which outlines the enabled faces faintly and draws a ring per blemish: click a ring to keep that spot, click skin to add one, ⌫ keeps the selected one. The faces are stored on the sensor grid, so crop, straighten, lens and perspective edits never lose them; the regions are built again from the image each time it opens (and shortly after a geometry edit), like model masks, rather than stored. When macOS runs critically short of memory the regions are let go and the panel says "Memory is low: touch-up will show again when memory recovers". Exports, prints, contact sheets and slides carry the same retouching; thumbnails and `latent-cli` renders leave out the smoothing, whitening and eyes (blemishes and dust are ordinary heal patches and render everywhere). Copy, paste and presets carry the sliders but not the faces; a pasted touch-up finds the faces of its new photo itself (see [Library](Library#applying-edits-to-many-images)).

**Local Adjustments.** Masks with their own sliders: linear gradient, radial, brush, whole image, colour and luminance ranges, and model masks (*Subject* with BiRefNet Lite or Apple Vision, *Click to Select* and *Select Objects* with Segment Anything 2.1, *Select by Class* with SegFormer). A mask can combine several parts (add, subtract, intersect), has an Amount, and offers Exposure, Contrast, Highlights, Shadows, Whites, Blacks, Temperature, Tint, Hue, Saturation, Texture, Clarity, Dehaze, Sharpness, Noise and Defringe, plus a Curve, Point Colour and Grain of its own; the brush has Auto Mask. Model masks take Feather and Edge, and masks can be renamed, duplicated, duplicated and inverted, hidden and tidied. Model masks are regenerated from the image when needed rather than stored, and each records the model that made it; see [Models](Models). Everything about masks is on its own page: [Masking](Masking).

**Distraction Removal.** **Reflections** takes the reflection of a window or glass pane off the scene behind it, with a reflection removal model you add yourself. See [Reflections](#reflections) below.

**Lens Blur.** A shallow depth of field after the fact, from a depth map made on this Mac: the background (or the foreground) goes soft while what you choose stays sharp. See [Lens Blur](#lens-blur) below.

**Tone Curve.** Two editors, picked with **Point** and **Region** at the top of the group.

*Point* is a master curve plus red, green and blue curves, picked with the RGB, Red, Green, Blue control above the graph; the channel curves apply after the master, on the display-referred image. Drag points, click the curve to add one, double-click a point to remove it. Channel curves you have changed stay faintly visible behind the one you are editing. Linear resets the curve shown. The **Point Curve** menu starts the RGB curve from **Linear**, **Medium Contrast** or **Strong Contrast**, Lightroom's three; it says Custom once you move a point.

*Region* is Lightroom's parametric curve: four sliders, **Highlights**, **Lights**, **Darks** and **Shadows**, each lifting or lowering one stretch of tones, with the ends pinned at black and white. The three triangles under the graph move where the stretches meet; drag one, or double-click it to put it back. The region curve applies first and the point curve after it, so the two combine; with the region curve in use, the Point editor says so. Neither can turn tones over: a strong negative slider flattens, it doesn't reverse.

**Refine Saturation**, under either editor, sets how much colour the curve's contrast brings. A steep curve applied to red, green and blue pulls them apart and saturates the picture; at −100 the curve changes brightness only and leaves colour as it was, at +100 it adds twice the colour. It does nothing while the curve is straight.

**Tone Equalizer.** Below the Tone Curve: nine bands of brightness moved through an edge-aware mask, darktable's tool for compressing a scene's range. See [Tone Equalizer](#tone-equalizer).

**Targeted adjustment.** The **Target** button in the Tone Curve group, and the **Hue**, **Saturation** and **Luminance** buttons above the HSL sliders, turn on a tool that works on the photo itself: press on a tone or colour and drag up to raise it, down to lower it. For the curve, the drag moves a point at that tone (an existing point if one is close, otherwise a new one) or, with Region showing, the region slider that tone falls in. For HSL, it moves the one or two hue bands that colour belongs to, in the proportion each acts on it. A whole drag is one step in history. Esc, or the same button, leaves the tool.

**HSL / Colour.** Hue, saturation and luminance for eight hue bands. Hold **⌥** while dragging one of the eight sliders to see only the colours it changes: the rest of the photo turns grey until you let go. It is on screen only.

**Black & White.** The **Treatment** switch at the top of the group turns the photo into **Color** or **Black & White**; **V** does the same, and so does Develop › Black & White. In black and white each pixel starts as its own luminance, and the **B&W Mix** below sets how light or dark each of the eight HSL colours turns: drag Blue down for a darker sky, Orange up for lighter skin. Greys stay the grey they were whatever the mix; the more colourful a pixel was, the more its slider moves it. **Auto** sets the mix for this photo so that each colour turns the grey it seems to have in colour (a saturated red looks brighter than a grey of the same luminance, and Auto keeps it so, which keeps a red flower apart from green leaves of the same brightness). The first time you switch a photo to Black & White, Auto sets the mix for you; after that the mix is yours, and it is kept when you switch back to Color. While the treatment is Black & White, HSL, Point Colour, Saturation and Vibrance do nothing; the tone controls, the curves, Colour Grading and Calibration's shadow tint all still work, so you can tone a black and white with the colour wheels. The built-in **Black & White** preset switches the treatment.

**Point Colour.** Changes one colour picked from the photo, and the colours near it. Click **Pick a Colour**, then click the photo: a swatch of that colour appears in the group (up to eight). With a swatch selected, **Hue** turns it and its neighbours round the colour circle (up to 60°), **Saturation** makes them greyer or more vivid, and **Luminance** darker or lighter. **Range** sets how different a colour may be from the picked one, in hue, saturation and brightness, and still change: low for one exact colour, high for a whole family of them. **Variance** works inside that range: below zero it pulls the colours together towards the picked one (blotchy skin or uneven foliage turns more even), above zero it spreads them further apart. The colour is read after the curves and HSL, so a swatch matches the photo you see. **Delete Swatch** removes the selected one. Point Colour has no effect in Black & White.

**Colour Grading.** Tints for the shadows, midtones and highlights, and a global one for the whole picture, on colour wheels. The angle round a wheel is the tint's colour and the distance from the centre its strength; drag anywhere on the wheel, double-click it to take the tint off. Hold **⇧** while dragging to keep the hue and change only the strength, and **⌘** to move the puck a tenth as far as the pointer, for fine changes; either can be pressed or let go during the drag. **3-Way** shows the shadow, midtone and highlight wheels together, each with a **luminance** slider under it that darkens or lightens that range; **Shadows**, **Midtones**, **Highlights** and **Global** each show one wheel, larger, with Hue, Saturation and Luminance sliders for exact values. **Blending** sets how far the ranges reach into each other: low for crisp divisions, high for broad, soft ones; 50 is where the old Split Toning panel always sat. **Balance** moves the line between shadows and highlights. Colour Grading replaced Split Toning: the shadow and highlight wheels are Split Toning's tints, so an edit made with Split Toning opens with its tints on those wheels and looks exactly as it did. In copy, paste and presets the group is called **Colour Grading & Split Toning**.

Under each 3-Way wheel's luminance slider is a smaller **saturation** slider for that range, also offered as **Range Saturation** in the single-wheel views: it makes that range less or more colourful by the same weights as the wheel. **Hue Shift**, under Balance, turns every colour round the colour circle after the tints, leaving greys grey. The small graph above Blending draws how far the shadows (blue), midtones (grey) and highlights (orange) reach across the tones from black to white, so Balance and Blending can be seen working, and **⌥-drag** a wheel's luminance slider to see that range on the photo itself, white where it acts fully and black where it doesn't.

**Calibration.** **Shadows Tint** adds green (left) or magenta (right) to the shadows, for a cast the white balance can't reach. **Red, Green and Blue Primary** each have a **Hue** and a **Saturation**: they move the camera's red, green or blue, and with it every colour made from it, the way a different camera profile would. Red Hue to the right turns reds towards orange and warms skin; Blue Saturation up deepens skies and enriches many other colours too. White and grey stay neutral whatever you set. Calibration is a group of its own in copy, paste and presets, and is copied by default.

Below the primaries, three additions from darktable's colour calibration, each off until you move it. **Gamut Compression** eases colours that lie beyond, or close to, the edge of what the working space holds back inside it, softly, so a neon sign or a saturated flower keeps its detail instead of flattening. **Highlight Purity** gives back the colour the tone curve bleaches out of the brightest tones, such as the orange of a sunset near the sun. **Adaptation** tells Latent what light the scene was lit by, a CIE standard illuminant (A for tungsten, D50 to D75 for daylight, F2, F7 and F11 for fluorescent tubes, LED-B1 to B5 for white LEDs, E) or a Custom chromaticity, and carries colours lit by it to how they would look under daylight (D65) with the chosen transform: **CAT16** (darktable's default), **Bradford** or **XYZ Scaling**. D65 changes nothing; **Hue** and **Chroma** fine-tune the chosen light. Adaptation works on top of the white balance, which stays as it is. All three travel with Calibration in copy, paste and presets.

**Channel Mixer.** Each output channel, Red, Green and Blue (the tabs), made from a mix of the red, green and blue the camera's colour gives, from −200% to +200% of each. **Normalise** scales each output's mix so it adds up to 100%, which keeps greys grey; **Monochrome** gives every output the same mix, a black and white made before any colour control sees the picture, which HSL and the B&W Mix then can't colour back. It works in linear light, folded into the camera's colour like Calibration, and travels with Calibration in copy, paste and presets.

**Lens Corrections.** Distortion, chromatic aberration and vignetting from a bundled Lensfun profile matched to the camera and lens, each switchable, plus manual distortion and vignetting, a purple/green defringe and a flat-field correction. See [Lens corrections in detail](Develop#lens-corrections-in-detail).

**Detail.** Demosaic algorithm (RCD, bilinear, or LMMSE for high-ISO files), sharpening with Amount, Radius, Threshold, Detail and Masking, and noise reduction with Luminance, Detail and Contrast, and Colour, Detail and Smoothness. See [Sharpening and noise reduction](Develop#sharpening-and-noise-reduction).

**Effects.** Post-crop vignetting and film grain, applied last, after sharpening. See [Effects](Develop#effects).

**AI Noise Reduction.** NAFNet, trained on real sensor noise, run once per image on the GPU (about 11 s for 24 MP) and then blended at any strength instantly. It runs in camera space before colour, so white balance and every later module see clean data. Exports run it again; thumbnails skip it. If macOS runs critically short of memory the result is let go, and it runs again once memory recovers.

**Soft Proof.** Preview the image as it will look as an sRGB or Display P3 file, or printed through a printer or paper ICC profile (see [Soft proofing](#soft-proofing) below).

## Soft proofing

Soft proofing shows on screen what a file or a print will look like before you make it. Open **Soft Proof** at the bottom of the adjustments and switch it on (or press **S** in Develop, Lightroom's key; **View › Soft Proofing** does the same), then pick the target from the menu under the switch:

- **sRGB** or **Display P3**, for a file.
- A profile under **Installed Profiles**: the printer, paper and CMYK profiles in the Mac's shared folders, /Library/ColorSync/Profiles and /System/Library/ColorSync/Profiles (and the folders printer installers make inside them).
- **Add Profile…** for a profile anywhere else. The file chooser opens in your own ~/Library/ColorSync/Profiles, where downloaded paper profiles often go. Latent can only read that folder once you have picked a profile there, so profiles in it are added one by one rather than listed. An added profile stays in the menu from then on, across launches; **Remove from Menu** forgets it (the file itself is left alone).

The target you pick is remembered for the next launch; Soft Proof itself always starts off. HDR highlights are switched off while proofing, since neither a print nor an SDR file can hold them.

**Intent** decides what happens to colours the target can't reproduce:

- **Perceptual** squeezes the whole image into the target's gamut, so colours keep their relationships but even the ones that would have fitted move a little. Best for photos with many saturated colours.
- **Relative Colorimetric** keeps every colour that fits exactly and clips the rest to the nearest one the target can make, with black point compensation, so the deepest black in the photo lands on the paper's deepest black and shadow detail isn't lost. Best when only a few colours are out of gamut.

- **Saturation** pushes colours to the most saturated the target can make, giving up accuracy. It is meant for charts and graphics rather than photos.
- **Absolute Colorimetric** keeps colours that fit exactly, the paper's own white included, without black point compensation: the proof is tinted by the paper. It is for proofing one paper or press on another.

Try Perceptual and Relative Colorimetric and keep the one that looks better; there is no rule. The intent only matters for ICC profiles: sRGB and Display P3 clip the same way either way.

**Black Point Compensation** (Relative Colorimetric with an ICC profile only, on by default) is the shadow mapping described above. Untick it to see what a conversion without it does: everything darker than the paper's deepest black blocks up into one black. A print made while proofing uses the same setting. Perceptual maps black to black by itself, so the box is greyed out there.

**Simulate Paper & Ink** (ICC profiles only) shows the paper's white and the ink's black as they really are, instead of mapping them to the screen's white and black. The proof goes duller and slightly tinted: that is what a print looks like next to a bright screen. Judge contrast and shadows with it on.

Two gamut warnings can paint over the proof:

- **Destination gamut warning** paints **red** the colours the target can't reproduce. Which colours are red doesn't depend on the intent.
- **Monitor gamut warning** paints **blue** the colours of the proof your screen itself can't show, so you know which parts of the proof can't be judged on this display. It uses the profile of the screen the window is on, which the panel names under the warnings ("Screen: Studio Display · Studio Display", say).

Each warning has its own colour well beside its box. Change a colour when the photo's own reds or blues would hide the warning, such as a red dress against the red warning; **Reset Warning Colours** brings back red and blue. The colours are remembered between launches. Where both warnings apply, the destination warning wins. The line under the panel says what share of all colours the target can't reproduce.

**Before shows the unproofed edit** (on by default): while proofing, **Before** (`\`) shows your edit as it is without the proof, so pressing `\` flips between the proof and the screen version, as Lightroom's Before/After does in Soft Proofing. Untick it and Before shows the photo before editing, as it does when you aren't proofing. Either way Before stays in SDR while proofing, so the two compare like with like.

**Create Proof Copy** keeps the edit as it is now as a snapshot named "Proof — " and the target's name, such as "Proof — Canson Rag Photographique". A proof copy is a snapshot, not a [virtual copy](Library#virtual-copies): the version adjusted for that paper, kept with the photo in the History panel's snapshot list, to restore before printing. To keep the screen version and the print version side by side as two photos, make a virtual copy (⌘') first and proof that. A typical session: save a snapshot of your screen version, switch on Soft Proof, adjust the photo until the proof looks right, then Create Proof Copy. Pressing it again for the same target replaces that proof copy (the button then reads **Update Proof Copy**). Like other snapshots, saving one isn't a step in History.

The target, intent, paper simulation and warnings are view settings, never part of the edit, and the intent and paper simulation are remembered between launches. Printing starts from the intent chosen here; see [Export](Export#print).

## HDR Limit

On an HDR screen, the **HDR Limit** slider under the **HDR display** switch sets how far above white this photo's highlights may go, from half a stop to three stops. The default is two stops, which is what Latent always used. Lower it for a portrait or a quiet scene whose highlights shouldn't glare; raise it for a sunset or specular light that deserves the sparkle. It is part of the edit: it is undoable, named "HDR Limit" in History, copied and pasted with the **Tone** group, and it sets the headroom of an exported HDR gain map, so the file looks on an HDR screen the way the photo looked while you edited it. It changes nothing in an SDR file or on an SDR screen. The slider only shows on a screen with HDR headroom; an edit's limit is kept and exported either way.

**Visualise HDR Ranges**, under the slider, shows where the photo uses that headroom: the SDR part of the picture turns a dim grey, and what lies above SDR white is coloured by how far, blue for the first stop, green for the second, yellow for the third and orange beyond, with magenta where it reaches the ceiling (the HDR Limit, or the screen's own headroom if that is lower), which is HDR clipping. It works with HDR display on, on screen only, and J leaves it alone.

## Tone Equalizer

darktable's signature tool: nine sliders, one for each stop of brightness from −8 EV (deep shadow) to 0 EV (the sensor's white), each raising or lowering the parts of the photo that sit at that brightness. What makes it different from a curve is the **mask** the bands are read from: the photo's brightness smoothed so that it follows edges. A whole sky, a whole shadowed face or a whole dark doorway gets one brightness, so the band that holds it moves all of it together and the texture inside it keeps its contrast, where a curve flattens it. Use it to bring a bright window and a dark room closer together, or to lift shadows without the grey, flat look Shadows can give when pushed hard.

It is a gain on all three channels at once, in linear light before the colour and tone stage, so it never shifts a colour, and it is off until a band leaves 0. The panel has three tabs:

- **Simple**: the nine bands as vertical sliders, −3 to +3 EV each. Drag up to brighten that tone, down to darken it; double-click a slider for 0. Grey bars behind the sliders show how much of the photo each band moves, so a slider with no bar has nothing to change; they follow Mask Exposure and Mask Contrast as you drag them.
- **Advanced**: the same nine values drawn as a curve over a histogram of the mask, so you can see which bands the photo actually uses. Drag a node up or down to move its band. Under it, **Mask histogram spread** says where the middle 90 % of the mask lies.
- **Masking**: how the mask is made. **Mask Exposure** slides it up or down the bands and **Mask Contrast** stretches or squeezes it about −4 EV, so a photo whose tones bunch into three or four bands can be spread across all nine; **Fit Mask** sets both for you, spreading the middle 90 % of the mask over the bands; **Auto Align** (on every tab) sets them so the bands span the photo's whole tonal range instead, its darkest tones (the 1st percentile of the mask) on the −8 EV band and its brightest (the 99th) on the 0 EV band. Neither moves a band. **Smoothing** sets how large an area the mask averages over (more keeps more local contrast) and **Edge Refinement** how closely it follows edges (more stops a change spilling across an edge as a halo).

Under every tab, **Show Mask** replaces the photo with the mask in grey, black at −8 EV to white at 0 EV, on screen only. While the panel is open, the colour readout under the histogram adds **Mask**, the mask's value under the pointer. **On Image** arms a targeted tool: scroll over the photo, or drag up or down on it, to raise or lower the band of the tone under the pointer; each scroll gesture or drag is one step in History. While it is armed, scrolling adjusts instead of panning (⌥ or ⌘ with the scroll still zooms); Esc puts it away.

The mask is made once for the whole photo, on a small copy about 512 pixels across, and every render reads that same mask with the full-resolution pixels refining its edges, so the 100 % view, the magnifier and an export show what the preview shows. Neither an exposure change nor a moved band remakes it. The histogram and the grey bars are worked out in the background after each render, from a summary of the mask made once each time it changes, so they keep up with a dragged slider without slowing it. Copy, paste and presets carry it as the **Tone Equalizer** group, which is part of the look and on by default; a preset's Amount blends the bands. History names it "Tone Equalizer".

## Lens Blur

A shallow depth of field after the fact: the background (or the foreground) goes soft while what you choose stays sharp, as with a fast lens wide open. It is off until **Amount** leaves 0; the first move makes a depth map of the photo on this Mac, which takes a moment, and nothing is sent anywhere.

- **Amount** (0–100) sets how large the blur grows. At 100 it reaches 2 % of the photo's long edge, about 120 pixels on a 24 MP frame.
- **Bokeh** is the shape out-of-focus highlights take: **Circle**, an even disc; **Bubble**, a disc with a bright rim, like older and mirror lenses draw; **5-Blade**, the pentagon of a lens with five straight aperture blades.
- **Type** (under Amount) is **Bokeh**, the disc described here and what every Lens Blur had before, **Gaussian**, a plain softening with no bright-edged discs, or **Motion**, a streak in one direction, as if the camera or the subject moved. For Gaussian, Amount is the blur's radius; for Motion, the streak's length, and **Angle** (0–179°, 0 horizontal, 90 vertical) its direction. Bokeh and Boost Highlights belong to Bokeh and are hidden for the other two. With a depth map the focus range keeps its slice of the scene sharp for all three; Gaussian and Motion also work without one, blurring the whole frame evenly (while the map is still being made, say, or where there is none, such as a thumbnail).
- **Boost Highlights** (0–100) brightens the brightest parts of the picture before they are blurred, so a lamp, a glint on water or the sun through leaves opens into a visible disc rather than fading to grey. Broad bright areas such as a pale sky brighten too; lower it if they wash out.
- **Focus Range** is the slice of the scene that stays sharp. The bar runs from far on the left to near on the right, in the colours Show Depth uses, with a pale histogram of how much of the photo lies at each distance. Drag either edge, or drag inside the light band to move the range without changing its width. Outside the range the blur grows with the distance from it, so a thin range gives a gentle fall-off and the far background is always fully blurred. VoiceOver reads it as two adjustable values, the far and the near edge.
- **Pick Focus** arms a one-click picker: click the part of the photo you want sharp and the focus range centres on its distance. Arming another tool, or Esc, cancels it.
- **Show Depth** replaces the photo with its depth map, amber near through teal to blue far, with the focus range washed light, so you can see what will stay sharp. It is for the screen only; the edit doesn't change.
- **Depth** names what made the depth map, and its menu makes it again with something else. With a depth model installed (Depth Anything V2 Small is in the catalogue; see [Models](Models#adding-a-model)) the map knows the whole scene, near ground to far hills. Without one, Latent uses two planes made from the subject mask (Settings › AI › Models picks which subject model): the subject is near and sharp, everything else is far and blurs evenly. That works well for a portrait or a single object against a background and less well for a scene that recedes, such as a road or a row of trees.

The blur works in linear light after the local adjustments, so a brightened background blurs brightened. Behind a sharp subject it never spreads across the subject's outline; a blurred foreground does overlap what is behind it, as it would through a lens. The depth map is made again after a lens or keystone change, and each time the photo opens, like model masks; the edit keeps only the sliders, the focus range and which depth source it was set on, so a photo edited on one Mac says what it needs on another, and one without that depth model uses the two planes and says so. Exports, prints, contact sheets and slides carry the blur; thumbnails and `latent-cli` renders leave it out. Copy, paste and presets carry it as the **Lens Blur** group, off by default like the masks.

## Reflections

The **Distraction Removal** group's **Reflections** slider (0 to 100) takes a reflection in a window, a shop front or a picture frame's glass off the scene behind it. 0 leaves the photo as shot; 100 takes the model's result whole; in between mixes the two.

It needs a reflection removal model, which you add yourself in Settings › AI › Models: none comes with the app, none is in the catalogue, and Latent never downloads one. `scripts/convert_reflection_removal.py` converts a network once someone has chosen one with a suitable licence and pinned its code and weights (see [Models](Models#reflection-removal)). Without a model the slider stays off and the panel says how to add one; there is no stand-in that would only pretend.

The model runs on this Mac when the amount first leaves 0, on the same analysis render Lens Blur's depth uses, and again after a lens or keystone change. What it returns becomes a gain per pixel and colour channel (the model's picture over the one it was given, in linear light), applied to the graded image straight after the colour stage and before Lens Blur. Going back to a photo whose map was made recently doesn't run the model again. The edit keeps only the amount and which model it was set on; a photo whose model this Mac lacks uses the installed one and says so. Exports, prints, contact sheets and slides carry it; thumbnails and `latent-cli` renders leave it out. Copy, paste and presets carry it with **Spot Removal & Red-Eye**.

## Remove and Detect Objects

**Remove** is the third mode, beside Heal and Clone, for taking something out of the picture: a sign, a stray branch, a person in the distance, a power line over a plain sky. It has no source. Click or brush over the thing (Spot or Brush, as for Heal), a little wider than it, and Remove fills that area with texture built from what is around it, carrying edges and gradients through, so a horizon or a fence line continues across the gap. The patch's outline is drawn dotted; drag it to move the patch, and the fill follows. Feather does nothing in Remove: the whole brushed area is replaced, with no fade. The fill is worked out again for every render, at the size of that render, so an export is filled at full resolution rather than scaled up from the preview; it is the same every time for the same patch. Remove builds the fill from the photo only, with no generative model: it handles skies, grass, water, walls, sand and other textures well, but cannot invent something that isn't there to copy, such as the hidden half of a face or a line of text. A Remove patch can cover at most 35% of the photo's short side; for a wire across the frame, use Heal with Brush. Switching a selected patch between Remove and Heal or Clone keeps it where it is; turned into Heal or Clone, it gets a source beside it.

**Detect Objects**, under the mode picker while Remove is on, makes Remove fill the object rather than everything you brushed. Brush roughly over the object, or click it: when you let go, the click-to-select model (Segment Anything 2.1, bundled, run on this Mac) looks at the area around the brush and finds the outline of the object under it, grown slightly so its soft edge and halo go too, and Remove then fills just that outline, keeping the background the brush also covered. The patch is filled from the brushed area at once and switches to the outline a moment later; the outline is tinted on the image. If nothing is found under the brush, or what is found is most of the area around it, the brushed area is kept and the status bar says so. With a Remove patch selected, **Detect Object** finds the outline again (after moving the patch, say) and **Use Brush** goes back to filling the brushed area. The outline is an edit like any other, so Undo (⌘Z) takes it back. The outline is stored with the edit, so exports, other Macs and `latent-cli` render it without the model. Detect Objects is unavailable when no click-to-select model is installed (see [Models](Models)).

### Fill and Blur

Two more modes for the Remove tool, on the **Paint** picker under Heal, Clone and Remove. Neither has a source: click or brush over the area, and drag the dotted outline to move it.

- **Fill** paints one colour over the patch, for a sensor spot on a plain sky, a blown highlight to tone down or a background to even out. Pick the **Colour** with the colour well and set **Brightness** in stops (−4 to +4). The colour goes through the photo's tone and colour edits like everything else, so it shows approximately; Brightness brings it into line.
- **Blur** softens what is under the patch by its **Blur Radius** (in sensor pixels), for skin, a busy patch of background or a sign you would rather keep but not read.

Both take the patch's Size, Feather and Opacity, work as a spot or a brush stroke, and are healed in camera space with the other patches, so they look the same at every zoom and in exports. The selected patch's settings show in the panel; with none selected, they are the next patch's.

## JPEG, TIFF and other pictures

JPEG, HEIC, TIFF, PNG, WebP, AVIF and JPEG XL files (the last three where macOS reads them) open in Develop like raws, and every edit is stored the same way, in the catalog and its sidecar, never in the file.

**They open looking exactly as they are.** Such a file was already developed by a camera, phone, scanner or another editor, so its colours and tones are finished. Latent converts it from its own colour profile (sRGB when it has none) into its working space and works out the light that its default tone curve would turn into those pixels, so the unedited picture shows as the file does and every slider moves from there. Exposure brightens in light rather than lifting a grey veil, and highlights roll off softly, as they do on a raw. White Balance starts at about 6500 K, meaning "as the file is": lower values cool the picture and higher ones warm it, relative to that.

**What is different from a raw:**

- **There is less to recover.** A JPEG keeps 8 bits per colour and its highlights are clipped where the camera clipped them, so big exposure or shadow pushes band and blow out sooner than on a raw. A 16-bit TIFF holds up much better.
- **Highlight Reconstruction is hidden.** It rebuilds clipped sensor channels, which a finished picture no longer has.
- **Lens Corrections has no profile toggles.** Profiles describe a raw's optics; a JPEG has usually been corrected by the camera or app that made it, and may be cropped. The manual distortion and vignetting sliders and Defringe still work.
- **Detail has no demosaic choice,** since there is no sensor mosaic. Sharpening and noise reduction work as usual.
- **AI Noise Reduction is not available.** The model is trained on raw sensor noise, which these files have already had processed away.
- **The view is SDR.** On an HDR screen the picture isn't stretched above paper white, and exports never get an HDR gain map. An HDR photo from a phone (a HEIC or JPEG with a gain map) opens as its ordinary SDR picture.
- **Photo Merge takes raws only,** so these files can't go into an HDR merge or a panorama.

The status bar shows the file's format and depth when it opens, such as "JPEG · 8-bit · FUJIFILM X-T5 · 6240×4160".

## Crop guides and the surround

While the crop tool is on, a composition guide is drawn inside the rectangle. **Overlay** in the panel picks it, and **O** cycles through them: Thirds, Grid, Golden Ratio (lines at 38% and 62%), Golden Spiral, Diagonal (45° from each corner), Triangle (a diagonal, with the perpendiculars to it from the other two corners) and None. **⇧O** turns the golden spiral through its four placements and swaps the triangle's diagonal. **Surround** sets how dark the picture outside the crop is drawn, from 0% (not darkened, to judge the crop against what you're leaving out) to 100% (black, to see the crop alone); double-click it for the usual 55%. Both are remembered between launches and change nothing in the photo.

## Transform and Upright

The Transform panel, under Crop and Straighten, corrects perspective and places the picture in the frame. Everything here is one geometric correction made in the same pass as the lens corrections, so it adds no rendering time, and it moves spots, red-eye fixes and faces with the picture. Its **Reset** puts Upright, the keystone and every slider back (Constrain Crop stays as you set it).

**Upright** finds the straight lines in the photo and corrects from them:

- **Auto** levels the picture and straightens converging verticals, but only part of the way when they lean strongly, which usually looks more natural than a full correction.
- **Level** only turns the picture, so the horizontal lines are level.
- **Vertical** makes the upright lines (walls, columns, trees) parallel and plumb, as if the camera hadn't been tilted up.
- **Full** does that for the horizontal lines too, as if the camera had faced the subject squarely. Strong corrections can stretch a picture; ease them with Aspect and Scale.
- **Guided** lets you show it the lines: with **Draw Guides** on, drag along two to four edges in the photo that should be upright or level. Two upright guides straighten converging verticals, two level ones straighten converging horizontals, and a single guide just levels or plumbs the picture by that line. The correction is made when you let go, and each guide is then drawn along its feature as it now stands. Drag a guide's end to move it, ⌥-click a guide to remove it, or **Clear** them all. Esc leaves the tool.

Upright works on the picture after its lens corrections (its lines are straight only then), so after changing the lens profile or distortion, press **Update** to read the lines again. To give a whole series the same mode, each with its own correction, copy with **Upright Mode** ticked and paste (see [Presets and clipboard](#presets-and-clipboard)). The correction it found is stored with the edit, so the photo looks the same on any Mac and in every export; nothing is sent anywhere. In a photo with few straight lines Upright may find nothing to correct, and says so in the status bar.

**Vertical** and **Horizontal** are manual keystone corrections, on top of Upright. **Rotate** turns the picture up to 10° either way, **Aspect** stretches it wider (positive) or taller (negative) without changing its area, **Scale** enlarges or shrinks it about the centre, and **X Offset** and **Y Offset** move it sideways and up or down. The sliders act on the picture as it looks after Upright and any flip.

**Flip Horizontal** and **Flip Vertical** (the two buttons, or Develop › Transform) mirror the photo, and its crop and masks with it, so what you see is exactly the mirror image of what was there. Flipping twice puts it back.

**Constrain Crop** keeps the crop inside the picture: whenever the transform or the straighten angle changes, the crop becomes the largest of its shape that shows no empty edge, and it grows back as the correction eases. With it off, the transform can show empty (black) areas at the edges, which you can crop by hand.

Develop › Transform has Auto Straighten, the flips, each Upright mode, and the crop guide commands.

## Hot pixels

Long exposures, astro frames and warm sensors show **hot pixels**: single photosites stuck bright, which demosaicing turns into small red, green or blue crosses. Sensor Dust doesn't catch them, since they are one pixel wide. **Detail › Hot Pixels › Remove hot pixels** compares every photosite with its eight nearest neighbours of the same colour on the raw sensor mosaic, before white balance and demosaicing, and replaces one that stands out with the median of those neighbours.

- **Stuck Pixels Only** (to start) replaces a photosite only when it is brighter than all eight neighbours, so a star or a glint that covers several pixels is left alone.
- **Also Dim Outliers** also takes a photosite well above its neighbours' median when one or two of them are bright too: clusters of warm pixels and the brightest specks of long exposure noise.
- **Threshold** (0–100, 50 to start) is how much brighter than its neighbours a photosite must be: lower finds more, higher only the most obvious.

The line under the slider says how many pixels the setting fixed in the whole frame ("1,204 pixels fixed"); it updates after the next render. The same pixels are replaced in the preview, at 100% and in exports. Hot Pixels travels with the **Detail** group in copy and paste and presets, and is greyed out for a JPEG, TIFF or merged DNG, which have no sensor mosaic left.

## Sharpening and noise reduction

The Detail panel's sliders, top to bottom. Judge them at 100% zoom (⌘1): the fitted view is scaled to predict the result but hides fine texture.

**Demosaic.** How full-resolution renders (100% and export) turn the sensor's colour mosaic into pixels. **RCD** is the default and the sharpest on clean files. **LMMSE (better for high ISO)** weighs a horizontal and a vertical estimate of each missing colour by how far each stands above its own noise, so noisy files get smooth, maze-free detail instead of the wormy patterns a gradient-led method can read into noise. **Bilinear** is fast and soft, kept for comparison. The fitted preview never demosaics, so the choice shows only at 100% and in exports.

**Sharpening.** **Amount** is how strongly edges are sharpened, 0 is off. **Radius** is the size of the detail sharpened, in sensor pixels. **Threshold** leaves detail fainter than it alone, so noise and grain aren't sharpened. **Detail** (0–100, 100 to start) controls halos: sharpening makes a bright rim on the light side of an edge and a dark one on the other. At 100 you get plain unsharp masking, as Latent always had; lower values pull the sharpened pixels back towards their neighbours' range, so edges still get crisper but without the rims. Try 25–50 for portraits and anything with bright edges against sky. **Masking** (0–100, 0 to start) limits sharpening to edges: at 0 everything is sharpened; as it rises, smooth areas such as sky, skin and out-of-focus backgrounds are left alone and only real edges sharpen. **Hold Option (⌥) while dragging the Masking slider** to see the mask itself in place of the photo: white is sharpened, black is left alone. Let go and the photo comes back. The preview is on screen only, never part of the edit.

**Noise reduction.** **Luminance** smooths brightness noise, the grainy speckle; **Colour** removes coloured speckles. Each has finer controls, greyed out while its slider is at 0:

- **Detail** under Luminance (0–100, 50 to start) sets what counts as noise. Higher keeps more fine texture (hair, fabric, foliage) at the cost of leaving some noise; lower smooths harder.
- **Contrast** under Luminance (0–100, 0 to start) puts back local contrast and texture the smoothing flattened, without bringing the speckle back with it. Push it on faces and fabrics that look waxy after noise reduction; too much can leave a blotchy look in very noisy shots.
- **Detail** under Colour (0–100, 50 to start) keeps thin coloured edges, such as a red jumper against a green hedge, from bleeding; lower values clean colour noise harder.
- **Smoothness** under Colour (0–100, 0 to start) evens out larger blotches of colour (mottling) in plain areas such as sky and shadows, which the Colour slider is too fine to reach.

At their starting values these sliders give exactly the noise reduction Latent always had, so an older edit looks the same.

## Lens corrections in detail

**Profile.** Under the description of the matched profile, a menu shows **Automatic profile**, or the lens picked by hand. Open it to choose any lens in the bundled Lensfun database: lenses that fit the camera's mount come first, under **Fits this camera**, then the rest, each grouped by maker. Use it when the automatic match is wrong, or when it found nothing because the camera didn't record enough about the lens (manual lenses and adapted lenses often don't). The choice belongs to this photo and is part of its edit, so ⌘Z takes it back; **Automatic** goes back to the match.

**Use for All Photos From This Lens** remembers the choice in the catalog for this camera and this lens, so every other photo taken with them uses it too, in the editor, in thumbnails and in exports. The photo's own choice then goes back to automatic, which now means the remembered profile. While a lens has a remembered profile and the photo is on automatic, the button reads **Forget Profile for This Lens** and goes back to the database's own match. Remembering is a catalog change: undo it with Edit › Undo in the Library. The choices are kept per catalog (each folder has its own), and `latent-cli` doesn't read them.

**Remove chromatic aberration (automatic).** Lateral chromatic aberration shows as red-cyan or blue-yellow fringes along edges towards the corners, because the lens enlarges each colour slightly differently. This switch measures it on the photo itself, without a profile: it looks at small patches across the frame, finds how far the red and blue edges sit from the green ones, and fits one correction for the whole lens. It takes a moment, then the panel says what it found ("Red +0.031%, blue −0.024% against green"). The measurement is stored in the edit, so the correction never changes afterwards, and exports match. While it is on it replaces the profile's chromatic aberration correction. A photo with few sharp edges (fog, a soft portrait background) may not have enough to measure: the panel says so and nothing changes.

**Manual chromatic aberration.** Under the automatic switch, **Red** and **Blue** (−0.50 to +0.50 %, 0 to start) line up each colour with green by hand: positive shrinks that colour's image towards the centre, pulling in fringes of that colour on the outer side of edges near the corners; negative enlarges it. They add to whatever correction is on (the profile's or the measured one), or work alone for a lens Lensfun doesn't know. Judge them at 100% on an edge near a corner. They travel with the **Lens Corrections** group.

**Manual.** **Distortion** corrects barrel or pincushion distortion by hand. **Vignetting** brightens (positive) or darkens (negative) the corners of the whole frame, the way the lens darkened them. **Midpoint** (0–100, 50 to start) sets how far in from the corners the manual vignetting reaches: lower spreads it towards the centre, higher keeps it in the corners. Manual vignetting is measured on the whole frame, before the crop; for a vignette that follows the crop, use [Effects](Develop#effects).

**Embedded DNG corrections.** Many phones and mirrorless cameras don't correct the raw data in their DNGs: they write the corrections into the file (its opcode lists) for the raw converter to apply, as lens-shading gain maps, radial vignetting and rectilinear distortion. For a DNG that carries any, the panel shows **Apply embedded DNG corrections** and says what the file corrects. Switched on, the vignetting and distortion (and, from per-colour warps, chromatic aberration) replace the Lensfun profile, still under the Distortion, Chromatic aberration and Vignetting switches, and the shading map is laid over the frame where a flat-field reference would be; a flat-field reference of your own, if the photo has one, takes its place. The switch is off to start, so photos you edited before stay as they were. Latent's lens models are smaller than the DNG's, so the corrections are fitted to them (see [Limitations](Limitations#editing)).

**Defringe.** **Purple** and **Green** remove purple-magenta and green-cyan fringes that sit along high-contrast edges, such as branches against a bright sky. Each has a hue range, shown as a strip of the colours it covers with two sliders for its ends, so you can widen it to catch bluer or pinker fringes, or narrow it to spare a purple flower. **Pick Fringe Colour** arms an eyedropper: click a fringe on the image (zoom in first) and the matching range, purple or green, moves to centre on its colour, and its amount comes on if it was off. Escape cancels the eyedropper. Defringe only touches colour on strong edges, so flat purple or green areas are left alone.

### Flat-field correction

A lens profile describes an average copy of a lens. A **flat-field reference** records your own: shoot a white diffuser held over the lens (or a plain, evenly lit wall or sky, slightly out of focus) with the same lens, focal length and aperture, exposed a stop or two brighter than the meter suggests. Its falloff towards the corners, and any colour cast across the frame (common with tilt-shift and wide-angle lenses on mirrorless bodies), are then taken out of every photo it is applied to.

**Photo › Flat-Field Correction…** applies one to the selection (in Loupe, Compare, Survey and Develop, the photo shown). Pick the reference among the selected photos, so selecting the reference frame with the photos it belongs to is all the marking needed (a file whose name contains "flat" is picked for you), or choose **Other File…**; set the **Amount**, and **Apply**. The reference is measured once: Latent renders it, averages it into a grid of 64 cells across, smooths it and works out a gain for each colour channel that brings every cell back to the centre's level. The job then writes the correction into each photo's edit as one undo step, "Flat-Field Correction (12 Images)", in the background like Remove Dust. A photo from a sensor of another shape is left alone and named in the notes; a reference too dark or clipped in the centre is refused with a message saying how to reshoot it.

The correction runs on the sensor grid in camera colour, after spot removal and before the lens corrections, so profile vignetting can still be switched on or off on top of it. In Lens Corrections, under **Flat-Field**, the panel shows the reference's name, an **Amount** slider (0–100) and **Remove**. The edit stores where the reference is, relative to the photo's folder, and the reference file's SHA-256, never the gain map: the map is kept in Application Support, and if it is missing (another Mac, say) it is made again from the reference when the photo opens or exports, provided the file found there is still the same one. If the reference has moved or changed the panel says so and the photo shows without the correction. Copy, paste and presets don't carry it, since the path belongs to the photo; apply it to more photos with the command.

## Effects

**Post-Crop Vignetting** darkens or lightens the edges of the picture as cropped, following the crop's centre, size and angle, so it always frames what you export. **Amount** darkens (negative) or lightens (positive); 0 is off. **Midpoint** sets how far in it reaches; **Roundness** goes from following the crop's rectangle (−100) through an oval fitting the crop (0) to a circle (+100); **Feather** softens its edge, 0 being a hard edge. **Highlights**, for darkening, lets bright areas such as lamps and sky keep their brightness. **Style**: *Highlight Priority* works like exposure, keeps highlights bright when darkening and rolls them gently into white when lightening, at the cost of slight colour shifts in bright areas; *Colour Priority* changes only brightness, keeping every pixel's hue and saturation, and never lightens a colour past white.

**Grain** adds film-like grain. **Amount** is its strength, 0 is off; **Size** the size of each grain relative to the picture, so a setting looks the same on a 12 and a 60 megapixel file; **Roughness** goes from smooth, even grain to clumpier, irregular grain. Grain is strongest in the midtones and gentler in deep shadows and bright highlights, as on film. The pattern is fixed to the photo: the same grains in the same places on screen, at every zoom, and in the export, and a different pattern for every photo. Zoomed out, the preview shows the grain softened, as a smaller print would; look at 100% to judge it.

Effects travel with copy, paste and presets as their own group, **Effects (post-crop vignetting, grain)**, on by default like the other look groups.

## Presets and clipboard

Presets and Clipboard holds a menu of presets, nine built in and any you save, and a checklist of which module groups copy and paste carry. **Crop & Straighten** carries the crop and angle; the transform has four groups of its own, as in Lightroom's Copy Settings: **Upright Mode** carries only the mode and runs its analysis again on each photo it is pasted onto, so every frame of a building gets its own correction; **Upright Transforms** carries the exact correction (and wins when both are ticked); **Transform Adjustments** carries the sliders and flips; **Constrain Crop** the switch. Guided Upright travels whole under either Upright group, since its guides were drawn by hand. Presets and checklists saved before these groups existed keep meaning what they did: Crop then carried the whole transform. The **Profile** group carries the chosen profile and its Amount, and is on by default. Spot removal patches and red-eye spots travel together, as **Spot Removal & Red-Eye**; **Sensor Dust**, **Touch-up** and **Lens Blur** are groups of their own, off by default like the masks, and Touch-up carries its sliders and Remove blemishes switch but never the faces or blemishes found on another photo. ⇧⌘C copies the current edit; ⇧⌘V pastes into the open image or onto the whole Library selection. Presets are JSON files in Application Support and can be shared.

**Amount**, under the preset menu, sets how strongly the next preset applies: 0% leaves the photo alone, 100% applies it as saved, 200% goes twice as far. Only slider values blend, from the photo's own value towards the preset's (exposure, contrast, the tone ranges, presence, vibrance and saturation, HSL, the curves, the strength of split toning and colour grading, Calibration, sharpening and noise reduction, grain and vignetting amounts, the tone equalizer's bands, white balance and a creative profile's Amount); switches, choices, hues, masks and crops come from the preset whole. Move the slider straight after applying a preset and that preset blends again live, as in Lightroom; once you change anything else, the slider only sets the strength of the next one. The Amount is remembered between launches; double-click it for 100%. Applied to a Library selection, white balance blends only where both the photo and the preset have a Temperature set; a photo still at As Shot takes the preset's.

Four of the built-in presets compress a scene's range. **Compress Shadows and Highlights (Soft)** and **(Strong)** lift the darker bands of the [Tone Equalizer](#tone-equalizer) and lower the brighter ones, and **Lift Shadows** only lifts; these three carry nothing but the tone equalizer, so they leave the rest of the edit alone. **Compress Range** does the same job with sliders, Highlights −40, Shadows +40 and a little Clarity; like the other slider presets it carries the whole Tone and Presence groups, so it also sets Exposure and the other tone sliders to their defaults.

**Previous** (the button beside **Reset All** at the foot of the panel, Edit › Paste Settings From Previous, ⌥⌘V) pastes the settings of the photo you selected just before this one, as Lightroom's Previous does. It carries every group except the retouching that belongs to one frame: spot removal and red-eye, sensor dust and touch-up. Like Paste it changes the open photo in Develop and the whole selection in the Library, with one undo step; a previous photo with no edit pastes the defaults. Opening another folder forgets the previous photo.

### Managing presets

A name with a **|** in it files the preset in a folder: **Film|Portra 400** appears as Portra 400 inside a Film submenu, in the Presets panel's menu, the Library panel's, the grid's right-click **Apply Preset** and **Develop › Apply Preset**. Folders can nest (**Film|Colour|Ektar**). Starred presets appear in a **Favourites** section at the top of those menus.

The **⋯** menu beside **Apply preset** holds **Manage Presets…**, **Import…** (one or more `.latentpreset` or `.json` files, copied into Latent; a name already taken gets a number, "Portra 2"), **Export** (one preset, or all of yours, to one file), **Favourite** (a tick per preset), **Duplicate**, **Rename** and **Update from Current Edit**, which captures the preset's groups again from the open photo and keeps its name, groups and description. The built-in presets can be starred, duplicated and exported, but not changed. Renaming a preset carries its star and its keyboard shortcut with it.

**Manage Presets…** lists every preset, grouped by folder, with a search field over names and descriptions. Choose one to star it, give a user preset a **Description**, untick groups it should no longer carry (a group it never saved can't be added here: use Update from Current Edit), or **Rename…**, **Duplicate**, **Export…**, **Update from Current Edit** and **Remove…** it. Photos a removed preset was applied to keep their settings.

**Apply modes.** Choosing a preset applies it over the photo's edit, as always. Hold **⌥** in an Apply Preset menu and each item becomes **(Overwrite)**: every photo starts from its default edit, so nothing of its old edit is left but what the preset sets (crop, masks and retouching included). **Apply as New Virtual Copy**, at the bottom of the menu, makes a virtual copy of each selected photo (the photo shown, in Develop) and applies the preset to the copies, leaving the originals alone; one Undo takes back both. Both use the Amount below.

**Panel presets.** The Tone, Tone Curve, HSL / Colour, Colour Grading, Calibration, Lens Corrections, Detail and Effects headers each have a small presets menu. It lists the presets that hold exactly that panel's group and applies them to the open photo at the Amount; **Save Panel as Preset…** saves just that panel's settings.

**Shortcuts for presets.** Each of your presets is listed in Settings › Keyboard, under **Presets**, as "Apply Preset: *name*", so it can be given a key. The key applies the preset to the selection in the Library and to the open photo in Develop, as the menu does. A key given to a preset that has since been deleted shows as **(missing)** and does nothing until a preset of that name exists again.

**Edit › Paste Settings (Overwrite)** pastes the same groups, but each photo first goes back to its defaults, so every group not pasted is reset rather than kept, as darktable's overwrite mode does. **Edit › Copy Settings…** and **Paste Settings…** ask which groups to take first, in a checklist with **All** and **None** that remembers its last choice (one for copying, one for pasting); a ticked group the clipboard doesn't set is put back to its default. The checklist under Presets and Clipboard, which ⇧⌘C and ⇧⌘V use, is left as it was. **File › Load Settings from File…** reads the edit from a Latent sidecar (`.xmp`), or from an image whose XMP carries Latent's edit, and applies the groups you tick to the open photo or the Library selection, as one undo step.

### Process versions

Each edit records the **process version** it was made with: which generation of Latent's rendering it uses. **Calibration › Process** shows it. When a future change to the rendering would make existing photos look different, it arrives as a new version: photos edited before keep rendering as they did, new edits use the new version, and editing an older photo offers **Update Process Version**, with a preview on the photo, **Update all filmstrip photos** (every photo the filmstrip shows, as one undo step) and **Don't show again** (the Process menu's **Offer to Update Older Photos** turns the offer back on). The Process menu also moves a photo between versions by hand.

There is only **Version 1** so far. The controls added in November 2026 changed nothing about an edit that doesn't use them, so every edit Latent has made renders as it always did and is Version 1; the dialog has nothing to offer until a Version 2 exists.

## History and snapshots

The left panel lists every settled edit newest first; click one to jump, ⌘Z and ⇧⌘Z step. Thirty steps are kept per image, in the sidecar as well as the catalog. Snapshots are named copies of the whole edit; restoring one is itself a history step. Steps are named by what changed, such as Tone, Saturation, Treatment, B&W Mix, Colour Grading, Calibration, Point Colour, Spot Removal, Red-Eye, Reflections, Sensor Dust, Touch-up, Lens Blur or HDR Limit; Find Spots, Find Faces and Find Blemishes are each one step.

**Compare as Before** (the split-square button on a snapshot's row, or its right-click menu) makes that snapshot the Before without touching the edit, as darktable compares a snapshot with the live edit. With one image showing, Develop switches to **Left / Right Split**; a Before/After layout already showing stays. The Before pane's label names the snapshot, as in "Before: Warm". **Clear Before** (the same button, the right-click menu, or the bar under the panes) goes back to the unedited photo.

## Before/After and Reference view

**Before/After.** `\` switches the whole image between the edit and the Before. **⇧Y** (View › Before/After) shows both at once and steps through Lightroom's four layouts: **Left / Right** (two panes side by side), **Left / Right Split** (one picture, the left half Before and the right half After), **Top / Bottom** and **Top / Bottom Split**, then back to one image. The bar under the panes picks a layout and has **Done**. Zoom and pan are linked: both halves always show the same part of the photo. The Before is drawn with the After's crop, straighten and transform, so the two line up as you crop; you keep editing the After with every tool, and in a split view a click on either half acts on the photo. The layout you used last is remembered. In a split, drag the line (the pointer turns into a resize arrow over it) to show more of one side; double-click it to put it back in the middle. VoiceOver can move it in steps of 5%.

The Before is the photo as it opened, unless you change it. **Copy After → Before** (⌥⇧⌘←) makes the current settings the Before, so you can compare the next round of changes with where you are now; **Copy Before → After** (⌥⇧⌘→) puts the Before's settings on the photo, as one step that ⌘Z takes back; **Swap** (⌥⇧⌘↑) exchanges them. **Reset Before** goes back to the unedited photo. A Before you set lasts until you open another photo or quit; it is not saved.

**Reference view.** **⇧R** (View › Reference View) puts a reference photo beside the one you edit, the Active photo, to match a set by eye. Drag a photo from the filmstrip onto the left pane to make it the reference, or choose **Set as Reference Photo** to make the photo you are editing the reference and then click another in the filmstrip. Entering Reference view with two photos selected makes the other one the reference. The bar under the panes picks **Left / Right** or **Top / Bottom**. The reference pane zooms and pans on its own and is for looking only; to edit it, press **Swap**, which opens it as the Active photo and makes the photo you were editing the reference. With **Lock** on, the reference stays when Reference view closes, and next time while the same folder is open; with Lock off it goes when Reference view closes.

## Loupe overlay

**View › Loupe Overlay** lays guides over the photo in Loupe and Develop, as Lightroom's does: a **Grid** of squares, two **Guides** (one across, one down) and a **Layout Image**. **Show Overlay** (⌥⌘O) shows or hides whichever of the three are on. While it shows, the small grid button at the top right of the image opens its settings: the grid's square size and opacity; Reset to put the guides back across the middle; and for the layout image its opacity, scale and position, and Fit to Photo. Drag a guide to move it; the guides keep their place in the picture as you zoom. **Choose Layout Image…** takes a PNG, typically one with transparency such as a magazine cover's masthead and cover lines, and lays it over the photo, fitted inside it; Latent keeps its own copy, so the original can move. The overlay is on screen only and never reaches an export.

## The viewport

Pinch, or scroll with ⌥ or ⌘, to zoom about the pointer; scroll or drag to pan a zoomed-in image; double-click, or double-tap with two fingers, to toggle fit and 100%. ⌘0 fits, ⌘1 is 100%, ⌘= and ⌘- step. At fit, a two-finger swipe steps to the next or previous image, and holding the mouse button shows a magnifier at 100% under the pointer; past 200% pixels are drawn as squares. F shows the image full screen, with the adjustments coming in from the right edge. See [Library](Library#looking-closely). The image surround grey is set in Settings. The filmstrip along the bottom opens another image with one click; see [Library](Library#filmstrip).

On an HDR display, highlights above paper white are shown using the display's headroom, up to the photo's [HDR Limit](#hdr-limit) (two stops unless you change it), with the HDR display switch to preview the SDR result. The image follows the screen's brightness without re-rendering, and the screen's extended range (which raises the backlight) is only switched on while the image needs it.

At the top of the right panel, a switch picks the scope: **Histogram**, **Waveform** (each channel's brightness against position across the image) or **Vectorscope** (colour, with neutral in the centre). Only the one shown is computed. The histogram draws red, green and blue with a luminance outline. Below it, the share of pixels clipped in the highlights and in the shadows per channel, and, with HDR display on, the share of the image above SDR white, which an SDR screen or file can't show. While a slider is dragged, the scopes update up to ten times a second.

**More scopes.** The button beside the switch offers three more: **RGB Parade**, the waveform's red, green and blue side by side, each in its own third with the same guide lines; **Waveform + Vectorscope** and **Histogram + Vectorscope**, two scopes stacked at half height, both measured from the same render. Choosing one leaves the switch's segments unselected; picking a segment goes back.

**Vectorscope guides.** Right-click the vectorscope for **Skin Tone Line**, a dashed line at about 123° where skin of every depth falls, and **Harmony** guides: Complementary, Split-complementary, Triad, Tetrad or Analogous, drawn as spokes with a narrow wedge either side. While a harmony shows, drag on the vectorscope to turn it to a hue; **Reset Harmony Angle** puts it back on the skin tone line. The same settings are in Preferences › Scopes. Guides are a display only.

**Dragging the histogram.** Hover over the histogram to see which slider each part of it drives, from left to right Blacks, Shadows, Exposure, Highlights and Whites, and drag sideways to move that slider: right raises it. The whole width is a slider's full travel, or four stops of exposure. Each drag is a step in history like a slider's.

**Colour under the pointer.** Under the scope, the colour beneath the pointer is read out as R, G and B in percent (as an encoded file would hold them) and as CIE Lab against a D50 white, the convention Photoshop and Lightroom use. It describes the picture as shown, after every adjustment. With HDR display on, a colour brighter than SDR white also reads how far above it it is, such as "+1.4 stops"; the RGB numbers stay on the 0–100 scale.

**Picker size and space.** The eyedropper button beside the readout sets the **Sample Size**, a single pixel (Point, as before) or the mean of a square 5, 15 or 31 image pixels across, which then also shows the least and greatest values on a second line; and **Show As**, the space the numbers are in: RGB % and Lab (as before), RGB %, RGB 0–255, HSL, LCh or Lab (D50). **Copy Value** copies the last reading over the photo as text. The settings are also in Preferences › Scopes.

**Pinned colour samples.** Option-click the photo in Develop (with no tool armed) to pin a sample there, up to eight, numbered on the photo. They are listed under the readout with their live values, which follow every edit, a swatch, and a button to remove each; right-click one to copy its value, or **Clear** to remove them all. Each is drawn as a numbered dot on the vectorscope (turn this off with **Show Pinned Samples** in its menu). Samples last for the session on that photo and are never saved with the edit.

**Clipping.** J (View › Show Clipping) paints clipped highlights red and crushed shadows blue on the image; the two buttons either side of the readout turn each on alone. Red marks a pixel with any channel at the top, blue a pixel whose every channel is at black, the same lines the clipped percentages count. With HDR display on, the top is the HDR ceiling rather than SDR white. The overlay is on screen only and never reaches an export.

**Clipping warning options.** The small arrow after the highlights button opens the warning's options: **Mode** (Any channel, as above; Luminance, the pixel's brightness against the lines; or Saturation (gamut), which marks a channel at the top while the colour is strong, or a colour outside Display P3, and a channel at black while another still has light), the **Shadows** line (0–5% of the range) and the **Highlights** line (95–100%), and the **Colours**: red and blue, magenta and cyan, or black and white (clipped highlights black, crushed shadows white). The defaults are the warning as described above, and Reset puts them back; the options are remembered between launches. The clipped percentages under the histogram keep their fixed lines.

**Raw clipping.** **View › Show Raw Clipping** paints magenta where the sensor itself clipped: a pixel any of whose channels reached the camera's white level, judged on the raw data before highlight reconstruction and the tone curve. Next to J it tells clipping you can't get back (magenta) from highlights the tone curve or exposure pushed over (red only). It is drawn from the same render as the picture, so zoomed in it is exact to the pixel; at fit the preview averages pixels together, so a few isolated clipped photosites may not show. It works in Develop on raws and merged DNGs (a JPEG or TIFF has no sensor data to judge) and never reaches an export.

**Focus peaking.** **View › Focus Peaking** draws the sharpest edges in the picture in colour, as a mirrorless viewfinder does, to see at a glance what is in focus. It works in Develop, Loupe, Compare, Survey and on a second display, at any zoom. Settings › Viewing sets its colour (red, green, yellow or white) and sensitivity (Low marks only the crispest edges, High softer ones too). It looks at the picture as shown, so heavy sharpening or noise adds marks, and it is best judged at fit or 100%.

**Colour assessment.** **View › Colour Assessment** shrinks the picture inside a wide border of 18% grey with a thin white frame around it, the viewing conditions ISO 12646 asks for when judging colour, as darktable's colour assessment mode does. Settings › Viewing sets the border, 5–20% of the view on each side (10% to start). It works in Develop, Loupe, Compare, Survey and on a second display; your own surround comes back when it is off.

**High quality preview.** **View › High Quality Preview** (off by default, remembered) draws the fitted view from a full-resolution render, demosaiced as an export is and shrunk with the export's Lanczos filter, instead of from the faster binned preview: finer detail and truer sharpening and noise at fit. It is made half a second after an edit, zoom or resize settles, so sliders stay as quick as ever, and zoomed in the full-resolution tile is already used. Each one briefly needs several hundred megabytes of graphics memory, which is why it is off by default; see Settings › Performance.

**Navigator and zoom levels.** **Navigator**, at the top of the right panel (closed until you open it, then remembered), shows the whole picture with the part in view outlined; click or drag in it to move the view there when zoomed in, and its Fit, Fill, 50%, 100% and 200% buttons set the zoom. View › Show Navigator opens or closes it, and shows it over the image in Loupe, Compare and Survey too. The arrow after the **100%** button in the status bar offers Fit, Fill (the picture filling the view, cropped at its edges), 25%, 50%, 100%, 200% and 400%; a level below Fit shows the picture fitted.

**Focus Exposure.** **Develop › Focus Exposure** opens the Tone panel and puts the keyboard in the Exposure value, ready to type a number (Return applies it, Esc cancels). It has no key until you give it one in Settings › Keyboard.
