# Develop

The right panel holds the modules in pipeline order. All controls are live at full quality. Double-click a slider to reset it. ⌘Z and ⇧⌘Z undo and redo; `\` shows the unedited image. The Develop menu holds Auto Adjust, the crop, spot removal, red-eye, sensor dust and touch-up tools, the White Balance Selector, new masks and brush size, and View › Show Clipping shows clipping on the image; the Edit menu holds undo, redo and Copy and Paste Settings.

**Typed values.** Most sliders show their value as a number you can click: type a value and press Return or Tab (or click elsewhere) to apply it, or Escape to cancel. If the value changes while the field is open, because the slider moved or another image opened, the field follows it and anything typed is dropped. ↑ and ↓ step it by the last digit shown, ⇧↑ and ⇧↓ by ten steps. The unit can be typed or left out, and the value is clamped to the slider's range. A typed value is an ordinary edit and lands in history like a drag. ## Modules

<!-- RECONCILE: two branches edited this; merge into one text -->
**White Balance.** Temperature and tint, starting from the camera's own setting. As Shot returns to it. Click the Kelvin number beside Temperature to type one (2000–12000 K).

The menu above the sliders offers Lightroom's starting points: **As Shot** (the camera's setting), **Auto** (an estimate from the picture, the same one Auto Adjust makes, without touching exposure or contrast), and the light sources **Daylight** (5500 K), **Cloudy** (6500 K), **Shade** (7500 K), **Tungsten** (2850 K), **Fluorescent** (3800 K, leaning a little magenta to cancel the green of the tubes) and **Flash** (5500 K). Moving a slider afterwards makes the setting **Custom**. Latent's tint scale is its own, so the numbers don't match Lightroom's.

**White Balance Selector.** Click the eyedropper, or press W, then click something in the photo that ought to be neutral: a grey card, a white wall, a cloud in shade. Temperature and tint change to make that spot grey, and the selector puts itself away. It reads a small patch of the raw data under the click, so it ignores the tone curve and every colour adjustment. A spot that is clipped, or too dark to have a reliable colour, is refused with a message in the status bar and the selector stays on for another try. Esc leaves it.

**Profile.** The row above White Balance sets the photo's starting colour and contrast, before any slider. Click the profile's name for the menu:

- **Latent Standard** is Latent's own rendering from the camera's colour matrix, the look every photo had before profiles existed. A photo that never had a profile chosen uses it, and always will.
- **Latent Neutral** has a little less contrast and gentler colour, a quiet starting point that leaves room to edit. **Latent Flat** goes further: very low contrast and muted colour, holding the most shadow and highlight detail, for grading.
- **Creative**: **Vivid** (more saturation and contrast, strongest on muted colours and a little easier on skin), **Portrait** (softer contrast, skin tones drawn together and a touch lighter and less red) and **Landscape** (richer foliage, deeper skies, a little more contrast).
- **Black & White**: **Monochrome** turns each colour into its brightness. The **Yellow**, **Red** and **Green Filter** versions work like a coloured filter on black and white film: yellow darkens blue skies a little, red darkens them a lot and pales skin, green lightens foliage and darkens reds and lips.
- **Camera Profiles for** your camera lists the DNG camera profiles (.dcp files) you have imported for it, and **Other Cameras** the rest, each with the camera it was made for. A profile made for another camera still works, but its colour is meant for that camera's sensor.

Creative and black and white profiles have an **Amount** slider, 0 to 200% (black and white to 100%): 0 is Latent Standard, 100% the look as designed, and above that the look is pushed further. Basic profiles and camera profiles have no Amount. Choosing a profile is an edit like any other: it lands in history, ⌘Z undoes it, and `\` shows the photo without it. Your sliders stay where they are when you switch, and work on top of the profile.

**Camera profiles (DCP).** A .dcp file describes how one camera model sees colour. You can get them from Adobe's free DNG Converter (which installs Adobe's profiles on your Mac), from some camera makers, or make your own from a colour chart with a tool such as dcamprof. Latent includes none and never downloads any. To add them, choose **Develop › Import Camera Profile…**, or **Import Profile…** at the bottom of the Profile menu, and pick one or more .dcp files; Latent checks that each is a readable profile and keeps a copy in its own profiles folder in Application Support, so the original can move or go. Importing the same file twice does nothing; a different file with the same name is kept as "Name 2".

Latent reads what makes a profile's colour: its colour matrices and forward matrices for one or two calibration lights, mixed for your white balance the way the DNG specification describes; its hue, saturation and value tables (including three-dimensional ones); its look table; and its exposure offset. With a camera profile chosen, its matrices replace the camera matrix Latent otherwise uses. White Balance still sets the camera's multipliers the way it always does, so a temperature can read a few kelvin differently from Adobe's for the same light.

A profile's own **tone curve** is off to start with: Latent keeps its own tone mapping (the sigmoid behind Contrast and Mid Grey, which holds highlights well past white) and takes only the profile's colour. A profile that carries a curve shows **Use profile's tone curve**; tick it to apply the curve on top of Latent's tone mapping, which is closer to the camera maker's contrast but usually stronger than in other editors, because those use the curve *instead* of a curve of their own.

A photo whose edit names a camera profile this Mac doesn't have (edited on another Mac, say) shows in Latent Standard, and the Profile row says which profile is missing. Import that profile and the photo looks as it was made. Copy and paste, presets and snapshots carry the profile as the **Profile** group.

**Tone.** Exposure in stops, contrast and mid-grey for the sigmoid tone curve. **Highlights, Shadows, Whites and Blacks** each brighten or darken one band of tones without moving mid grey. They change exposure per pixel by its brightness, so colours keep their hue, there are no halos, and tones never swap order. **Auto** (⌘U) sets exposure, contrast and white balance from the image itself, backing exposure off if it would blow the brightest highlights. On an HDR screen, **HDR display** shows highlights above paper white using the screen's headroom.

**Presence.** Texture (fine local contrast, 1–4 px), Clarity (mid-scale, midtone-weighted), Dehaze (dark-channel prior in linear light; negative adds haze), Vibrance (saturation that favours muted colours and spares skin) and **Saturation**, which moves every colour by the same proportion: −1 takes all the colour out, +1 doubles it.

**Crop and Straighten.** R opens the tool: drag inside to move, edges and corners to resize, with aspect presets that follow the image orientation (1:1, 3:2, 4:3, 5:4, 5:7, 16:9 and their tall forms, the original's own, or **Custom…**, which takes any width and height, such as 1.85 × 1). Straighten runs −45° to 45° and the crop shrinks to avoid empty corners, growing back as the angle returns. **Auto** levels the picture by the straight lines in it: horizons, rooflines, door frames, the edges of a building. To straighten by a line of your own, hold ⌘ and drag along something that should be level or upright with the crop tool on (or click the ruler button, then drag): when you let go, the picture turns so that line is level, or plumb if it was nearer upright. The crop is a sampling map, not a pipeline stage, so it costs no re-render. **Reset** removes the crop and straighten; the Transform panel has its own Reset. See [Crop guides and the surround](#crop-guides-and-the-surround) and [Transform and Upright](#transform-and-upright) below.

**Spot Removal.** H arms the tool. Click a spot to place a patch with an automatic source; drag from the click to choose the source; drag either circle to move it; ⌫ deletes the selected patch. [ and ] make the next patch, and the selected one, smaller or larger. **Heal** copies the source's texture and takes tone and colour from what surrounds the target on every side, so a gradient or an edge running through the patch carries on. **Clone** copies exactly. Patches apply in order, each seeing the ones before, so a patch next to an earlier one matches it. Up to 32 patches.

**Shape** switches between **Spot** and **Brush**. With Brush, drag along a wire, a hair or a dust streak: it becomes one patch when you let go, with its source placed beside it, across the stroke. Drag the dashed outline or its dot to move the source, or the solid outline to move the stroke. A click with Brush makes an ordinary circle. [ and ] resize the selected patch. A stroke counts as one of the 32 patches, however long, and keeps up to 256 points; a longer scribble is simplified to fit. Healing copies the source's texture, so pick a source without edges running along the stroke: a cloud edge or a horizon under the source shows up as a line.

**Remove** is the third mode, beside Heal and Clone, for taking something out of the picture: a sign, a stray branch, a person in the distance, a power line over a plain sky. It has no source. Click or brush over the thing (Spot or Brush, as for Heal), a little wider than it, and Remove fills that area with texture built from what is around it, carrying edges and gradients through, so a horizon or a fence line continues across the gap. The patch's outline is drawn dotted; drag it to move the patch, and the fill follows. Feather does nothing in Remove: the whole brushed area is replaced, with no fade. The fill is worked out again for every render, at the size of that render, so an export is filled at full resolution rather than scaled up from the preview; it is the same every time for the same patch. Remove builds the fill from the photo only, with no generative model: it handles skies, grass, water, walls, sand and other textures well, but cannot invent something that isn't there to copy, such as the hidden half of a face or a line of text. A Remove patch can cover at most 35% of the photo's short side; for a wire across the frame, use Heal with Brush. Switching a selected patch between Remove and Heal or Clone keeps it where it is; turned into Heal or Clone, it gets a source beside it.

**Detect Objects**, under the mode picker while Remove is on, makes Remove fill the object rather than everything you brushed. Brush roughly over the object, or click it: when you let go, the click-to-select model (Segment Anything 2.1, bundled, run on this Mac) looks at the area around the brush and finds the outline of the object under it, grown slightly so its soft edge and halo go too, and Remove then fills just that outline, keeping the background the brush also covered. The patch is filled from the brushed area at once and switches to the outline a moment later; the outline is tinted on the image. If nothing is found under the brush, or what is found is most of the area around it, the brushed area is kept and the status bar says so. With a Remove patch selected, **Detect Object** finds the outline again (after moving the patch, say) and **Use Brush** goes back to filling the brushed area. The outline is an edit like any other, so Undo (⌘Z) takes it back. The outline is stored with the edit, so exports, other Macs and `latent-cli` render it without the model. Detect Objects is unavailable when no click-to-select model is installed (see [Models](Models)).

**Red-Eye**, under Spot Removal. Y arms the tool. Click a red pupil to add a spot, and drag while adding to size it; drag inside a spot to move it and its rim to resize it. ⌫ removes the selected spot, and [ and ] resize it. **Pupil Size** sets the selected spot's size (and the next one's), and **Darken** its strength. **Auto** finds faces with Apple's Vision framework on this Mac, with nothing sent anywhere, and adds a spot for each pupil that is actually red; brown and dark eyes are left alone. Only flash-red pixels inside a circle change, so a generous circle is harmless. Spots follow crop, straighten and rotation. Up to 32 spots.

**Highlight Reconstruction.** Recovery and threshold pull clipped channels back toward neutral before the colour matrix. This repairs clipped colour; it doesn't move tones, which is what Highlights and Whites do.

**Sensor Dust**, under Red-Eye. Dust on the sensor shows as faint round shadows in plain areas, the same spots in every photo, clearest at small apertures (f/11 and beyond). **Find Spots** looks for them on this Mac and heals each one, replacing whatever the last Find Spots found; the status bar says "Found 37 dust spots", and the count line under the controls says how many there are. **Sensitivity** (0–100, 50 to start) trades fainter and less round spots against false ones, and **Spot Size** (Small, Medium or Large: shadows 4–8, 6–16 or 12–40 pixels across on the sensor) says what to look for; the band is narrowed to what the photo's aperture and sensor predict, and moving either re-detects at once while the tool is on. **Spots…** (Develop › Sensor Dust) arms the tool and shows each spot as a thin ring: click a ring to remove a false spot, click the image to add one, ⌫ deletes the selected ring. **Clear** removes them all. **Visualise Spots** shows the photo as a high-pass view in which dust shadows stand out, on screen only, never in an export; **Contrast** sets how faint a shadow still shows. The spots are heal patches, healed ahead of your own patches, so a spot you also patched by hand takes the hand patch on top; Find Spots skips spots already under one. Before (`\`) shows the photo without them. Up to 200 spots per photo. **Save as dust map…** keeps the current spots as a map for this camera ("Nikon D750 · 12 Sep 2026 · 41 spots"), and **From dust map…** looks for a saved map's spots in this photo and heals the ones that are there; a map from another camera, or another sensor size, is refused. To remove dust from many photos at once, or to make a map from a reference photo, use Photo › Remove Dust… (see [Library](Library#remove-dust)). Auto Adjust (⌘U) never runs Find Spots.

**Touch-up**, under Sensor Dust, retouches faces: **Find Faces** finds them on this Mac with Apple's Vision framework, nothing sent anywhere, and lists each with a thumbnail and a switch ("Face 1", numbered left to right) so a face can be left alone; faces narrower than 64 pixels are counted "too small to retouch". **Skin Smoothing** softens the skin's fine texture and keeps its shape; **Teeth Whitening** takes the yellow out of the teeth; **Brighten Eyes** lifts the whites and the irises. All three run 0–100 and do nothing until a face is found and on. The skin, teeth and eye regions come from the face's landmarks and a skin-colour test, so brows, lips, eyes and nostrils are left out of the smoothing; **Show Skin Mask** tints the skin the sliders work on. **Remove blemishes** heals the spots **Find Blemishes** finds on the enabled faces' skin: dark or reddish round marks, up to 64 per photo, listed with a count and a mini Clear; switch it off to keep the list without healing. **Touch-up…** (Develop › Touch-up) arms the tool, which outlines the enabled faces faintly and draws a ring per blemish: click a ring to keep that spot, click skin to add one, ⌫ keeps the selected one. The faces are stored on the sensor grid, so crop, straighten, lens and perspective edits never lose them; the regions are built again from the image each time it opens (and shortly after a geometry edit), like model masks, rather than stored. When macOS runs critically short of memory the regions are let go and the panel says "Memory is low: touch-up will show again when memory recovers". Exports, prints, contact sheets and slides carry the same retouching; thumbnails and `latent-cli` renders leave out the smoothing, whitening and eyes (blemishes and dust are ordinary heal patches and render everywhere). Copy, paste and presets carry the sliders but not the faces; a pasted touch-up finds the faces of its new photo itself (see [Library](Library#applying-edits-to-many-images)).

**Local Adjustments.** Masks with their own sliders: linear gradient, radial, brush, whole image, colour and luminance ranges, and model masks (*Subject* with BiRefNet Lite or Apple Vision, *Click to Select* and *Select Objects* with Segment Anything 2.1, *Select by Class* with SegFormer). A mask can combine several parts (add, subtract, intersect), has an Amount, and offers Exposure, Contrast, Highlights, Shadows, Whites, Blacks, Temperature, Tint, Hue, Saturation, Texture, Clarity, Dehaze, Sharpness, Noise and Defringe. Model masks take Feather and Edge, and masks can be renamed, duplicated, duplicated and inverted, hidden and tidied. Model masks are regenerated from the image when needed rather than stored, and each records the model that made it; see [Models](Models). Everything about masks is on its own page: [Masking](Masking).

**Tone Curve.** Two editors, picked with **Point** and **Region** at the top of the group.

*Point* is a master curve plus red, green and blue curves, picked with the RGB, Red, Green, Blue control above the graph; the channel curves apply after the master, on the display-referred image. Drag points, click the curve to add one, double-click a point to remove it. Channel curves you have changed stay faintly visible behind the one you are editing. Linear resets the curve shown. The **Point Curve** menu starts the RGB curve from **Linear**, **Medium Contrast** or **Strong Contrast**, Lightroom's three; it says Custom once you move a point.

*Region* is Lightroom's parametric curve: four sliders, **Highlights**, **Lights**, **Darks** and **Shadows**, each lifting or lowering one stretch of tones, with the ends pinned at black and white. The three triangles under the graph move where the stretches meet; drag one, or double-click it to put it back. The region curve applies first and the point curve after it, so the two combine; with the region curve in use, the Point editor says so. Neither can turn tones over: a strong negative slider flattens, it doesn't reverse.

**Refine Saturation**, under either editor, sets how much colour the curve's contrast brings. A steep curve applied to red, green and blue pulls them apart and saturates the picture; at −100 the curve changes brightness only and leaves colour as it was, at +100 it adds twice the colour. It does nothing while the curve is straight.

**Targeted adjustment.** The **Target** button in the Tone Curve group, and the **Hue**, **Saturation** and **Luminance** buttons above the HSL sliders, turn on a tool that works on the photo itself: press on a tone or colour and drag up to raise it, down to lower it. For the curve, the drag moves a point at that tone (an existing point if one is close, otherwise a new one) or, with Region showing, the region slider that tone falls in. For HSL, it moves the one or two hue bands that colour belongs to, in the proportion each acts on it. A whole drag is one step in history. Esc, or the same button, leaves the tool.

**Lens Blur.** A shallow depth of field after the fact: the background (or the foreground) goes soft while what you choose stays sharp, as with a fast lens wide open. It is off until **Amount** leaves 0; the first move makes a depth map of the photo on this Mac, which takes a moment, and nothing is sent anywhere.

- **Amount** (0–100) sets how large the blur grows. At 100 it reaches 2 % of the photo's long edge, about 120 pixels on a 24 MP frame.
- **Bokeh** is the shape out-of-focus highlights take: **Circle**, an even disc; **Bubble**, a disc with a bright rim, like older and mirror lenses draw; **5-Blade**, the pentagon of a lens with five straight aperture blades.
- **Boost Highlights** (0–100) brightens the brightest parts of the picture before they are blurred, so a lamp, a glint on water or the sun through leaves opens into a visible disc rather than fading to grey. Broad bright areas such as a pale sky brighten too; lower it if they wash out.
- **Focus Range** is the slice of the scene that stays sharp. The bar runs from far on the left to near on the right, in the colours Show Depth uses, with a pale histogram of how much of the photo lies at each distance. Drag either edge, or drag inside the light band to move the range without changing its width. Outside the range the blur grows with the distance from it, so a thin range gives a gentle fall-off and the far background is always fully blurred. VoiceOver reads it as two adjustable values, the far and the near edge.
- **Pick Focus** arms a one-click picker: click the part of the photo you want sharp and the focus range centres on its distance. Arming another tool, or Esc, cancels it.
- **Show Depth** replaces the photo with its depth map, amber near through teal to blue far, with the focus range washed light, so you can see what will stay sharp. It is for the screen only; the edit doesn't change.
- **Depth** names what made the depth map, and its menu makes it again with something else. With a depth model installed (Depth Anything V2 Small is in the catalogue; see [Models](Models#adding-a-model)) the map knows the whole scene, near ground to far hills. Without one, Latent uses two planes made from the subject mask (Settings › AI › Models picks which subject model): the subject is near and sharp, everything else is far and blurs evenly. That works well for a portrait or a single object against a background and less well for a scene that recedes, such as a road or a row of trees.

The blur works in linear light after the local adjustments, so a brightened background blurs brightened. Behind a sharp subject it never spreads across the subject's outline; a blurred foreground does overlap what is behind it, as it would through a lens. The depth map is made again after a lens or keystone change, and each time the photo opens, like model masks; the edit keeps only the sliders, the focus range and which depth source it was set on, so a photo edited on one Mac says what it needs on another, and one without that depth model uses the two planes and says so. Exports, prints, contact sheets and slides carry the blur; thumbnails and `latent-cli` renders leave it out. Copy, paste and presets carry it as the **Lens Blur** group, off by default like the masks.

**Tone Curve.** A master curve plus red, green and blue curves, picked with the RGB, Red, Green, Blue control above the graph; the channel curves apply after the master, on the display-referred image. Drag points, click the curve to add one, double-click a point to remove it. Channel curves you have changed stay faintly visible behind the one you are editing. Linear resets the curve shown.

**HSL / Colour.** Hue, saturation and luminance for eight hue bands.

**Black & White.** The **Treatment** switch at the top of the group turns the photo into **Color** or **Black & White**; **V** does the same, and so does Develop › Black & White. In black and white each pixel starts as its own luminance, and the **B&W Mix** below sets how light or dark each of the eight HSL colours turns: drag Blue down for a darker sky, Orange up for lighter skin. Greys stay the grey they were whatever the mix; the more colourful a pixel was, the more its slider moves it. **Auto** sets the mix for this photo so that each colour turns the grey it seems to have in colour (a saturated red looks brighter than a grey of the same luminance, and Auto keeps it so, which keeps a red flower apart from green leaves of the same brightness). The first time you switch a photo to Black & White, Auto sets the mix for you; after that the mix is yours, and it is kept when you switch back to Color. While the treatment is Black & White, HSL, Point Colour, Saturation and Vibrance do nothing; the tone controls, the curves, Colour Grading and Calibration's shadow tint all still work, so you can tone a black and white with the colour wheels. The built-in **Black & White** preset switches the treatment.

**Point Colour.** Changes one colour picked from the photo, and the colours near it. Click **Pick a Colour**, then click the photo: a swatch of that colour appears in the group (up to eight). With a swatch selected, **Hue** turns it and its neighbours round the colour circle (up to 60°), **Saturation** makes them greyer or more vivid, and **Luminance** darker or lighter. **Range** sets how different a colour may be from the picked one, in hue, saturation and brightness, and still change: low for one exact colour, high for a whole family of them. **Variance** works inside that range: below zero it pulls the colours together towards the picked one (blotchy skin or uneven foliage turns more even), above zero it spreads them further apart. The colour is read after the curves and HSL, so a swatch matches the photo you see. **Delete Swatch** removes the selected one. Point Colour has no effect in Black & White.

**Colour Grading.** Tints for the shadows, midtones and highlights, and a global one for the whole picture, on colour wheels. The angle round a wheel is the tint's colour and the distance from the centre its strength; drag anywhere on the wheel, double-click it to take the tint off. **3-Way** shows the shadow, midtone and highlight wheels together, each with a **luminance** slider under it that darkens or lightens that range; **Shadows**, **Midtones**, **Highlights** and **Global** each show one wheel, larger, with Hue, Saturation and Luminance sliders for exact values. **Blending** sets how far the ranges reach into each other: low for crisp divisions, high for broad, soft ones; 50 is where the old Split Toning panel always sat. **Balance** moves the line between shadows and highlights. Colour Grading replaced Split Toning: the shadow and highlight wheels are Split Toning's tints, so an edit made with Split Toning opens with its tints on those wheels and looks exactly as it did. In copy, paste and presets the group is called **Colour Grading & Split Toning**.

**Calibration.** **Shadows Tint** adds green (left) or magenta (right) to the shadows, for a cast the white balance can't reach. **Red, Green and Blue Primary** each have a **Hue** and a **Saturation**: they move the camera's red, green or blue, and with it every colour made from it, the way a different camera profile would. Red Hue to the right turns reds towards orange and warms skin; Blue Saturation up deepens skies and enriches many other colours too. White and grey stay neutral whatever you set. Calibration is a group of its own in copy, paste and presets, and is copied by default.

**Lens Corrections.** Distortion, chromatic aberration and vignetting from a bundled Lensfun profile matched to the camera and lens, each switchable, plus manual distortion and vignetting and a purple/green defringe. See [Lens corrections in detail](Develop#lens-corrections-in-detail).

**Detail.** Demosaic algorithm (RCD or bilinear), sharpening with Amount, Radius, Threshold, Detail and Masking, and noise reduction with Luminance, Detail and Contrast, and Colour, Detail and Smoothness. See [Sharpening and noise reduction](Develop#sharpening-and-noise-reduction).

**Effects.** Post-crop vignetting and film grain, applied last, after sharpening. See [Effects](Develop#effects).

**AI Noise Reduction.** NAFNet, trained on real sensor noise, run once per image on the GPU (about 11 s for 24 MP) and then blended at any strength instantly. It runs in camera space before colour, so white balance and every later module see clean data. Exports run it again; thumbnails skip it. If macOS runs critically short of memory the result is let go, and it runs again once memory recovers.

**Soft Proof.** Preview the image as sRGB or Display P3, or through an ICC profile, with a gamut warning that paints out-of-range colours grey.

## JPEG, TIFF and other pictures

JPEG, HEIC, TIFF, PNG, WebP and AVIF files open in Develop like raws, and every edit is stored the same way, in the catalog and its sidecar, never in the file.

**They open looking exactly as they are.** Such a file was already developed by a camera, phone, scanner or another editor, so its colours and tones are finished. Latent converts it from its own colour profile (sRGB when it has none) into its working space and works out the light that its default tone curve would turn into those pixels, so the unedited picture shows as the file does and every slider moves from there. Exposure brightens in light rather than lifting a grey veil, and highlights roll off softly, as they do on a raw. White Balance starts at about 6500 K, meaning "as the file is": lower values cool the picture and higher ones warm it, relative to that.

**What is different from a raw:**

- **There is less to recover.** A JPEG keeps 8 bits per colour and its highlights are clipped where the camera clipped them, so big exposure or shadow pushes band and blow out sooner than on a raw. A 16-bit TIFF holds up much better.
- **Highlight Reconstruction is hidden.** It rebuilds clipped sensor channels, which a finished picture no longer has.
- **Lens Corrections has no profile toggles.** Profiles describe a raw's optics; a JPEG has usually been corrected by the camera or app that made it, and may be cropped. The manual distortion and vignetting sliders and Defringe still work.
- **Detail has no demosaic choice,** since there is no sensor mosaic. Sharpening and noise reduction work as usual.
- **AI Noise Reduction is not available.** The model is trained on raw sensor noise, which these files have already had processed away.
- **The view is SDR.** On an HDR screen the picture isn't stretched above paper white, and exports never get an HDR gain map. An HDR photo from a phone (a HEIC or JPEG with a gain map) opens as its ordinary SDR picture.
- **Photo Merge takes raws only,** so these files can't go into an HDR merge or a panorama.

The status bar shows the file's format and depth when it opens, such as "JPEG · 8-bit · FUJIFILM X-T5 · 6240×4160".

## Crop guides and the surround

While the crop tool is on, a composition guide is drawn inside the rectangle. **Overlay** in the panel picks it, and **O** cycles through them: Thirds, Grid, Golden Ratio (lines at 38% and 62%), Golden Spiral, Diagonal (45° from each corner), Triangle (a diagonal, with the perpendiculars to it from the other two corners) and None. **⇧O** turns the golden spiral through its four placements and swaps the triangle's diagonal. **Surround** sets how dark the picture outside the crop is drawn, from 0% (not darkened, to judge the crop against what you're leaving out) to 100% (black, to see the crop alone); double-click it for the usual 55%. Both are remembered between launches and change nothing in the photo.

## Transform and Upright

The Transform panel, under Crop and Straighten, corrects perspective and places the picture in the frame. Everything here is one geometric correction made in the same pass as the lens corrections, so it adds no rendering time, and it moves spots, red-eye fixes and faces with the picture. Its **Reset** puts Upright, the keystone and every slider back (Constrain Crop stays as you set it).

**Upright** finds the straight lines in the photo and corrects from them:

- **Auto** levels the picture and straightens converging verticals, but only part of the way when they lean strongly, which usually looks more natural than a full correction.
- **Level** only turns the picture, so the horizontal lines are level.
- **Vertical** makes the upright lines (walls, columns, trees) parallel and plumb, as if the camera hadn't been tilted up.
- **Full** does that for the horizontal lines too, as if the camera had faced the subject squarely. Strong corrections can stretch a picture; ease them with Aspect and Scale.
- **Guided** lets you show it the lines: with **Draw Guides** on, drag along two to four edges in the photo that should be upright or level. Two upright guides straighten converging verticals, two level ones straighten converging horizontals, and a single guide just levels or plumbs the picture by that line. The correction is made when you let go, and each guide is then drawn along its feature as it now stands. Drag a guide's end to move it, ⌥-click a guide to remove it, or **Clear** them all. Esc leaves the tool.

Upright works on the picture after its lens corrections (its lines are straight only then), so after changing the lens profile or distortion, press **Update** to read the lines again. The correction it found is stored with the edit, so the photo looks the same on any Mac and in every export; nothing is sent anywhere. In a photo with few straight lines Upright may find nothing to correct, and says so in the status bar.

**Vertical** and **Horizontal** are manual keystone corrections, on top of Upright. **Rotate** turns the picture up to 10° either way, **Aspect** stretches it wider (positive) or taller (negative) without changing its area, **Scale** enlarges or shrinks it about the centre, and **X Offset** and **Y Offset** move it sideways and up or down. The sliders act on the picture as it looks after Upright and any flip.

**Flip Horizontal** and **Flip Vertical** (the two buttons, or Develop › Transform) mirror the photo, and its crop and masks with it, so what you see is exactly the mirror image of what was there. Flipping twice puts it back.

**Constrain Crop** keeps the crop inside the picture: whenever the transform or the straighten angle changes, the crop becomes the largest of its shape that shows no empty edge, and it grows back as the correction eases. With it off, the transform can show empty (black) areas at the edges, which you can crop by hand.

Develop › Transform has Auto Straighten, the flips, each Upright mode, and the crop guide commands.

## Sharpening and noise reduction

The Detail panel's sliders, top to bottom. Judge them at 100% zoom (⌘1): the fitted view is scaled to predict the result but hides fine texture.

**Sharpening.** **Amount** is how strongly edges are sharpened, 0 is off. **Radius** is the size of the detail sharpened, in sensor pixels. **Threshold** leaves detail fainter than it alone, so noise and grain aren't sharpened. **Detail** (0–100, 100 to start) controls halos: sharpening makes a bright rim on the light side of an edge and a dark one on the other. At 100 you get plain unsharp masking, as Latent always had; lower values pull the sharpened pixels back towards their neighbours' range, so edges still get crisper but without the rims. Try 25–50 for portraits and anything with bright edges against sky. **Masking** (0–100, 0 to start) limits sharpening to edges: at 0 everything is sharpened; as it rises, smooth areas such as sky, skin and out-of-focus backgrounds are left alone and only real edges sharpen. **Hold Option (⌥) while dragging the Masking slider** to see the mask itself in place of the photo: white is sharpened, black is left alone. Let go and the photo comes back. The preview is on screen only, never part of the edit.

**Noise reduction.** **Luminance** smooths brightness noise, the grainy speckle; **Colour** removes coloured speckles. Each has finer controls, greyed out while its slider is at 0:

- **Detail** under Luminance (0–100, 50 to start) sets what counts as noise. Higher keeps more fine texture (hair, fabric, foliage) at the cost of leaving some noise; lower smooths harder.
- **Contrast** under Luminance (0–100, 0 to start) puts back local contrast and texture the smoothing flattened, without bringing the speckle back with it. Push it on faces and fabrics that look waxy after noise reduction; too much can leave a blotchy look in very noisy shots.
- **Detail** under Colour (0–100, 50 to start) keeps thin coloured edges, such as a red jumper against a green hedge, from bleeding; lower values clean colour noise harder.
- **Smoothness** under Colour (0–100, 0 to start) evens out larger blotches of colour (mottling) in plain areas such as sky and shadows, which the Colour slider is too fine to reach.

At their starting values these sliders give exactly the noise reduction Latent always had, so an older edit looks the same.

## Lens corrections in detail

**Profile.** Under the description of the matched profile, a menu shows **Automatic profile**, or the lens picked by hand. Open it to choose any lens in the bundled Lensfun database: lenses that fit the camera's mount come first, under **Fits this camera**, then the rest, each grouped by maker. Use it when the automatic match is wrong, or when it found nothing because the camera didn't record enough about the lens (manual lenses and adapted lenses often don't). The choice belongs to this photo and is part of its edit, so ⌘Z takes it back; **Automatic** goes back to the match.

**Use for All Photos From This Lens** remembers the choice in the catalog for this camera and this lens, so every other photo taken with them uses it too, in the editor, in thumbnails and in exports. The photo's own choice then goes back to automatic, which now means the remembered profile. While a lens has a remembered profile and the photo is on automatic, the button reads **Forget Profile for This Lens** and goes back to the database's own match. Remembering is a catalog change: undo it with Edit › Undo in the Library. The choices are kept per catalog (each folder has its own), and `latent-cli` doesn't read them.

**Remove chromatic aberration (automatic).** Lateral chromatic aberration shows as red-cyan or blue-yellow fringes along edges towards the corners, because the lens enlarges each colour slightly differently. This switch measures it on the photo itself, without a profile: it looks at small patches across the frame, finds how far the red and blue edges sit from the green ones, and fits one correction for the whole lens. It takes a moment, then the panel says what it found ("Red +0.031%, blue −0.024% against green"). The measurement is stored in the edit, so the correction never changes afterwards, and exports match. While it is on it replaces the profile's chromatic aberration correction. A photo with few sharp edges (fog, a soft portrait background) may not have enough to measure: the panel says so and nothing changes.

**Manual.** **Distortion** corrects barrel or pincushion distortion by hand. **Vignetting** brightens (positive) or darkens (negative) the corners of the whole frame, the way the lens darkened them. **Midpoint** (0–100, 50 to start) sets how far in from the corners the manual vignetting reaches: lower spreads it towards the centre, higher keeps it in the corners. Manual vignetting is measured on the whole frame, before the crop; for a vignette that follows the crop, use [Effects](Develop#effects).

**Defringe.** **Purple** and **Green** remove purple-magenta and green-cyan fringes that sit along high-contrast edges, such as branches against a bright sky. Each has a hue range, shown as a strip of the colours it covers with two sliders for its ends, so you can widen it to catch bluer or pinker fringes, or narrow it to spare a purple flower. **Pick Fringe Colour** arms an eyedropper: click a fringe on the image (zoom in first) and the matching range, purple or green, moves to centre on its colour, and its amount comes on if it was off. Escape cancels the eyedropper. Defringe only touches colour on strong edges, so flat purple or green areas are left alone.

## Effects

**Post-Crop Vignetting** darkens or lightens the edges of the picture as cropped, following the crop's centre, size and angle, so it always frames what you export. **Amount** darkens (negative) or lightens (positive); 0 is off. **Midpoint** sets how far in it reaches; **Roundness** goes from following the crop's rectangle (−100) through an oval fitting the crop (0) to a circle (+100); **Feather** softens its edge, 0 being a hard edge. **Highlights**, for darkening, lets bright areas such as lamps and sky keep their brightness. **Style**: *Highlight Priority* works like exposure, keeps highlights bright when darkening and rolls them gently into white when lightening, at the cost of slight colour shifts in bright areas; *Colour Priority* changes only brightness, keeping every pixel's hue and saturation, and never lightens a colour past white.

**Grain** adds film-like grain. **Amount** is its strength, 0 is off; **Size** the size of each grain relative to the picture, so a setting looks the same on a 12 and a 60 megapixel file; **Roughness** goes from smooth, even grain to clumpier, irregular grain. Grain is strongest in the midtones and gentler in deep shadows and bright highlights, as on film. The pattern is fixed to the photo: the same grains in the same places on screen, at every zoom, and in the export, and a different pattern for every photo. Zoomed out, the preview shows the grain softened, as a smaller print would; look at 100% to judge it.

Effects travel with copy, paste and presets as their own group, **Effects (post-crop vignetting, grain)**, on by default like the other look groups.

## The viewport

Pinch, or scroll with ⌥ or ⌘, to zoom about the pointer; scroll or drag to pan a zoomed-in image; double-click, or double-tap with two fingers, to toggle fit and 100%. ⌘0 fits, ⌘1 is 100%, ⌘= and ⌘- step. At fit, a two-finger swipe steps to the next or previous image, and holding the mouse button shows a magnifier at 100% under the pointer; past 200% pixels are drawn as squares. F shows the image full screen, with the adjustments coming in from the right edge. See [Library](Library#looking-closely). The image surround grey is set in Settings. The filmstrip along the bottom opens another image with one click; see [Library](Library#filmstrip).

On an HDR display, highlights above paper white are shown using the display's headroom, up to two stops, with the HDR display switch to preview the SDR result. The image follows the screen's brightness without re-rendering, and the screen's extended range (which raises the backlight) is only switched on while the image needs it.

At the top of the right panel, a switch picks the scope: **Histogram**, **Waveform** (each channel's brightness against position across the image) or **Vectorscope** (colour, with neutral in the centre). Only the one shown is computed. The histogram draws red, green and blue with a luminance outline. Below it, the share of pixels clipped in the highlights and in the shadows per channel, and, with HDR display on, the share of the image above SDR white, which an SDR screen or file can't show. While a slider is dragged, the scopes update up to ten times a second.

**Dragging the histogram.** Hover over the histogram to see which slider each part of it drives, from left to right Blacks, Shadows, Exposure, Highlights and Whites, and drag sideways to move that slider: right raises it. The whole width is a slider's full travel, or four stops of exposure. Each drag is a step in history like a slider's.

**Colour under the pointer.** Under the scope, the colour beneath the pointer is read out as R, G and B in percent (as an encoded file would hold them) and as CIE Lab against a D50 white, the convention Photoshop and Lightroom use. It describes the picture as shown, after every adjustment.

**Clipping.** J (View › Show Clipping) paints clipped highlights red and crushed shadows blue on the image; the two buttons either side of the readout turn each on alone. Red marks a pixel with any channel at the top, blue a pixel whose every channel is at black, the same lines the clipped percentages count. With HDR display on, the top is the HDR ceiling rather than SDR white. The overlay is on screen only and never reaches an export.
