# Develop

The right panel holds the modules in pipeline order. All controls are live at full quality. Double-click a slider to reset it. ⌘Z and ⇧⌘Z undo and redo; `\` shows the unedited image. The Develop menu holds Auto Adjust, the crop, spot removal, red-eye, sensor dust and touch-up tools, new masks and brush size; the Edit menu holds undo, redo and Copy and Paste Settings.

**Typed values.** Most sliders show their value as a number you can click: type a value and press Return or Tab (or click elsewhere) to apply it, or Escape to cancel. If the value changes while the field is open, because the slider moved or another image opened, the field follows it and anything typed is dropped. ↑ and ↓ step it by the last digit shown, ⇧↑ and ⇧↓ by ten steps. The unit can be typed or left out, and the value is clamped to the slider's range. A typed value is an ordinary edit and lands in history like a drag. Temperature, the four tone ranges, HSL, the B&W Mix, the luminance sliders under the three small colour wheels and export quality don't take typed values yet.

## Modules

**White Balance.** Temperature and tint, starting from the camera's own setting. As Shot returns to it.

**Tone.** Exposure in stops, contrast and mid-grey for the sigmoid tone curve. **Highlights, Shadows, Whites and Blacks** each brighten or darken one band of tones without moving mid grey. They change exposure per pixel by its brightness, so colours keep their hue, there are no halos, and tones never swap order. **Auto** (⌘U) sets exposure, contrast and white balance from the image itself, backing exposure off if it would blow the brightest highlights. On an HDR screen, **HDR display** shows highlights above paper white using the screen's headroom.

**Presence.** Texture (fine local contrast, 1–4 px), Clarity (mid-scale, midtone-weighted), Dehaze (dark-channel prior in linear light; negative adds haze), Vibrance (saturation that favours muted colours and spares skin) and **Saturation**, which moves every colour by the same proportion: −1 takes all the colour out, +1 doubles it.

**Crop and Straighten.** R opens the tool: drag inside to move, edges and corners to resize, with aspect presets that follow the image orientation. Straighten runs −45° to 45° and the crop shrinks to avoid empty corners, growing back as the angle returns. Perspective offers vertical and horizontal keystone. The crop is a sampling map, not a pipeline stage, so it costs no re-render.

**Spot Removal.** H arms the tool. Click a spot to place a patch with an automatic source; drag from the click to choose the source; drag either circle to move it; ⌫ deletes the selected patch. [ and ] make the next patch, and the selected one, smaller or larger. **Heal** copies the source's texture and takes tone and colour from what surrounds the target on every side, so a gradient or an edge running through the patch carries on. **Clone** copies exactly. Patches apply in order, each seeing the ones before, so a patch next to an earlier one matches it. Up to 32 patches.

**Shape** switches between **Spot** and **Brush**. With Brush, drag along a wire, a hair or a dust streak: it becomes one patch when you let go, with its source placed beside it, across the stroke. Drag the dashed outline or its dot to move the source, or the solid outline to move the stroke. A click with Brush makes an ordinary circle. [ and ] resize the selected patch. A stroke counts as one of the 32 patches, however long, and keeps up to 256 points; a longer scribble is simplified to fit. Healing copies the source's texture, so pick a source without edges running along the stroke: a cloud edge or a horizon under the source shows up as a line.

**Red-Eye**, under Spot Removal. Y arms the tool. Click a red pupil to add a spot, and drag while adding to size it; drag inside a spot to move it and its rim to resize it. ⌫ removes the selected spot, and [ and ] resize it. **Pupil Size** sets the selected spot's size (and the next one's), and **Darken** its strength. **Auto** finds faces with Apple's Vision framework on this Mac, with nothing sent anywhere, and adds a spot for each pupil that is actually red; brown and dark eyes are left alone. Only flash-red pixels inside a circle change, so a generous circle is harmless. Spots follow crop, straighten and rotation. Up to 32 spots.

**Highlight Reconstruction.** Recovery and threshold pull clipped channels back toward neutral before the colour matrix. This repairs clipped colour; it doesn't move tones, which is what Highlights and Whites do.

**Sensor Dust**, under Red-Eye. Dust on the sensor shows as faint round shadows in plain areas, the same spots in every photo, clearest at small apertures (f/11 and beyond). **Find Spots** looks for them on this Mac and heals each one, replacing whatever the last Find Spots found; the status bar says "Found 37 dust spots", and the count line under the controls says how many there are. **Sensitivity** (0–100, 50 to start) trades fainter and less round spots against false ones, and **Spot Size** (Small, Medium or Large: shadows 4–8, 6–16 or 12–40 pixels across on the sensor) says what to look for; the band is narrowed to what the photo's aperture and sensor predict, and moving either re-detects at once while the tool is on. **Spots…** (Develop › Sensor Dust) arms the tool and shows each spot as a thin ring: click a ring to remove a false spot, click the image to add one, ⌫ deletes the selected ring. **Clear** removes them all. **Visualise Spots** shows the photo as a high-pass view in which dust shadows stand out, on screen only, never in an export; **Contrast** sets how faint a shadow still shows. The spots are heal patches, healed ahead of your own patches, so a spot you also patched by hand takes the hand patch on top; Find Spots skips spots already under one. Before (`\`) shows the photo without them. Up to 200 spots per photo. **Save as dust map…** keeps the current spots as a map for this camera ("Nikon D750 · 12 Sep 2026 · 41 spots"), and **From dust map…** looks for a saved map's spots in this photo and heals the ones that are there; a map from another camera, or another sensor size, is refused. To remove dust from many photos at once, or to make a map from a reference photo, use Photo › Remove Dust… (see [Library](Library#remove-dust)). Auto Adjust (⌘U) never runs Find Spots.

**Touch-up**, under Sensor Dust, retouches faces: **Find Faces** finds them on this Mac with Apple's Vision framework, nothing sent anywhere, and lists each with a thumbnail and a switch ("Face 1", numbered left to right) so a face can be left alone; faces narrower than 64 pixels are counted "too small to retouch". **Skin Smoothing** softens the skin's fine texture and keeps its shape; **Teeth Whitening** takes the yellow out of the teeth; **Brighten Eyes** lifts the whites and the irises. All three run 0–100 and do nothing until a face is found and on. The skin, teeth and eye regions come from the face's landmarks and a skin-colour test, so brows, lips, eyes and nostrils are left out of the smoothing; **Show Skin Mask** tints the skin the sliders work on. **Remove blemishes** heals the spots **Find Blemishes** finds on the enabled faces' skin: dark or reddish round marks, up to 64 per photo, listed with a count and a mini Clear; switch it off to keep the list without healing. **Touch-up…** (Develop › Touch-up) arms the tool, which outlines the enabled faces faintly and draws a ring per blemish: click a ring to keep that spot, click skin to add one, ⌫ keeps the selected one. The faces are stored on the sensor grid, so crop, straighten, lens and perspective edits never lose them; the regions are built again from the image each time it opens (and shortly after a geometry edit), like model masks, rather than stored. When macOS runs critically short of memory the regions are let go and the panel says "Memory is low: touch-up will show again when memory recovers". Exports, prints, contact sheets and slides carry the same retouching; thumbnails and `latent-cli` renders leave out the smoothing, whitening and eyes (blemishes and dust are ordinary heal patches and render everywhere). Copy, paste and presets carry the sliders but not the faces; a pasted touch-up finds the faces of its new photo itself (see [Library](Library#applying-edits-to-many-images)).

**Local Adjustments.** Masks with exposure, contrast, saturation and warmth applied inside them: linear gradient, radial, brush, whole image, and three kinds of model mask. The brush has size, feather and flow; [ and ] change its size while painting or erasing. *Subject* selects the main subject in one go, with no clicks, using BiRefNet Lite (bundled) or Apple Vision; *Click to Select* uses Segment Anything 2.1: click the subject, option-click to exclude. The Add menu lists the installed models of each kind, the default first; a mask's row names its model and its Model menu makes it again with another. *Select by Class* uses SegFormer to pick sky, people, vegetation, water, buildings, ground, mountains, animals or vehicles. Any mask can be refined by a luminance or hue range and inverted. Model masks are regenerated from the image when needed rather than stored, and each records the model that made it; see [Models](Models) for what happens on a Mac without that model, and for adding models.

**Tone Curve.** A master curve plus red, green and blue curves, picked with the RGB, Red, Green, Blue control above the graph; the channel curves apply after the master, on the display-referred image. Drag points, click the curve to add one, double-click a point to remove it. Channel curves you have changed stay faintly visible behind the one you are editing. Linear resets the curve shown.

**HSL / Colour.** Hue, saturation and luminance for eight hue bands.

**Black & White.** The **Treatment** switch at the top of the group turns the photo into **Color** or **Black & White**; **V** does the same, and so does Develop › Black & White. In black and white each pixel starts as its own luminance, and the **B&W Mix** below sets how light or dark each of the eight HSL colours turns: drag Blue down for a darker sky, Orange up for lighter skin. Greys stay the grey they were whatever the mix; the more colourful a pixel was, the more its slider moves it. **Auto** sets the mix for this photo so that each colour turns the grey it seems to have in colour (a saturated red looks brighter than a grey of the same luminance, and Auto keeps it so, which keeps a red flower apart from green leaves of the same brightness). The first time you switch a photo to Black & White, Auto sets the mix for you; after that the mix is yours, and it is kept when you switch back to Color. While the treatment is Black & White, HSL, Point Colour, Saturation and Vibrance do nothing; the tone controls, the curves, Colour Grading and Calibration's shadow tint all still work, so you can tone a black and white with the colour wheels. The built-in **Black & White** preset switches the treatment.

**Point Colour.** Changes one colour picked from the photo, and the colours near it. Click **Pick a Colour**, then click the photo: a swatch of that colour appears in the group (up to eight). With a swatch selected, **Hue** turns it and its neighbours round the colour circle (up to 60°), **Saturation** makes them greyer or more vivid, and **Luminance** darker or lighter. **Range** sets how different a colour may be from the picked one, in hue, saturation and brightness, and still change: low for one exact colour, high for a whole family of them. **Variance** works inside that range: below zero it pulls the colours together towards the picked one (blotchy skin or uneven foliage turns more even), above zero it spreads them further apart. The colour is read after the curves and HSL, so a swatch matches the photo you see. **Delete Swatch** removes the selected one. Point Colour has no effect in Black & White.

**Colour Grading.** Tints for the shadows, midtones and highlights, and a global one for the whole picture, on colour wheels. The angle round a wheel is the tint's colour and the distance from the centre its strength; drag anywhere on the wheel, double-click it to take the tint off. **3-Way** shows the shadow, midtone and highlight wheels together, each with a **luminance** slider under it that darkens or lightens that range; **Shadows**, **Midtones**, **Highlights** and **Global** each show one wheel, larger, with Hue, Saturation and Luminance sliders for exact values. **Blending** sets how far the ranges reach into each other: low for crisp divisions, high for broad, soft ones; 50 is where the old Split Toning panel always sat. **Balance** moves the line between shadows and highlights. Colour Grading replaced Split Toning: the shadow and highlight wheels are Split Toning's tints, so an edit made with Split Toning opens with its tints on those wheels and looks exactly as it did. In copy, paste and presets the group is called **Colour Grading & Split Toning**.

**Calibration.** **Shadows Tint** adds green (left) or magenta (right) to the shadows, for a cast the white balance can't reach. **Red, Green and Blue Primary** each have a **Hue** and a **Saturation**: they move the camera's red, green or blue, and with it every colour made from it, the way a different camera profile would. Red Hue to the right turns reds towards orange and warms skin; Blue Saturation up deepens skies and enriches many other colours too. White and grey stay neutral whatever you set. Calibration is a group of its own in copy, paste and presets, and is copied by default.

**Lens Corrections.** Distortion, chromatic aberration and vignetting from a bundled Lensfun profile matched to the camera and lens, each switchable, plus manual distortion and vignetting and a purple/green defringe.

**Detail.** Demosaic algorithm (RCD or bilinear), sharpening (amount, radius, threshold on perceptual luminance), and bilateral noise reduction in camera space.

**AI Noise Reduction.** NAFNet, trained on real sensor noise, run once per image on the GPU (about 11 s for 24 MP) and then blended at any strength instantly. It runs in camera space before colour, so white balance and every later module see clean data. Exports run it again; thumbnails skip it. If macOS runs critically short of memory the result is let go, and it runs again once memory recovers.

**Soft Proof.** Preview the image as sRGB or Display P3, or through an ICC profile, with a gamut warning that paints out-of-range colours grey.

## JPEG, TIFF and other pictures

JPEG, HEIC, TIFF, PNG, WebP and AVIF files open in Develop like raws, and every edit is stored the same way, in the catalog and its sidecar, never in the file.

**They open looking exactly as they are.** Such a file was already developed by a camera, phone, scanner or another editor, so its colours and tones are finished. Latent converts it from its own colour profile (sRGB when it has none) into its working space and works out the light that its default tone curve would turn into those pixels, so the unedited picture shows as the file does and every slider moves from there. Exposure brightens in light rather than lifting a grey veil, and highlights roll off softly, as they do on a raw. White Balance starts at about 6500 K, meaning "as the file is": lower values cool the picture and higher ones warm it, relative to that.

**What is different from a raw:**

- **There is less to recover.** A JPEG keeps 8 bits per colour and its highlights are clipped where the camera clipped them, so big exposure or shadow pushes band and blow out sooner than on a raw. A 16-bit TIFF holds up much better.
- **Highlight Reconstruction is hidden.** It rebuilds clipped sensor channels, which a finished picture no longer has.
- **Lens Corrections has no profile toggles.** Profiles describe a raw's optics; a JPEG has usually been corrected by the camera or app that made it, and may be cropped. The manual distortion and vignetting sliders and Defringe still work.
- **Detail has no demosaic choice,** since there is no sensor mosaic. Sharpening and noise reduction work as usual.
- **AI Noise Reduction is not available.** The model is trained on raw sensor noise, which these files have already had processed away.
- **The view is SDR.** On an HDR screen the picture isn't stretched above paper white, and exports never get an HDR gain map. An HDR photo from a phone (a HEIC or JPEG with a gain map) opens as its ordinary SDR picture.
- **Photo Merge takes raws only,** so these files can't go into an HDR merge or a panorama.

The status bar shows the file's format and depth when it opens, such as "JPEG · 8-bit · FUJIFILM X-T5 · 6240×4160".

## Presets and clipboard

Presets and Clipboard holds a menu of presets, five built in and any you save, and a checklist of which module groups copy and paste carry. Spot removal patches and red-eye spots travel together, as **Spot Removal & Red-Eye**; **Sensor Dust** and **Touch-up** are groups of their own, off by default like the masks, and Touch-up carries its sliders and Remove blemishes switch but never the faces or blemishes found on another photo. ⇧⌘C copies the current edit; ⇧⌘V pastes into the open image or onto the whole Library selection. Presets are JSON files in Application Support and can be shared.

## History and snapshots

The left panel lists every settled edit newest first; click one to jump, ⌘Z and ⇧⌘Z step. Thirty steps are kept per image, in the sidecar as well as the catalog. Snapshots are named copies of the whole edit; restoring one is itself a history step. Steps are named by what changed, such as Tone, Saturation, Treatment, B&W Mix, Colour Grading, Calibration, Point Colour, Spot Removal, Red-Eye, Sensor Dust or Touch-up; Find Spots, Find Faces and Find Blemishes are each one step.

## The viewport

Pinch, or scroll with ⌥ or ⌘, to zoom about the pointer; scroll or drag to pan a zoomed-in image; double-click, or double-tap with two fingers, to toggle fit and 100%. ⌘0 fits, ⌘1 is 100%, ⌘= and ⌘- step. At fit, a two-finger swipe steps to the next or previous image, and holding the mouse button shows a magnifier at 100% under the pointer; past 200% pixels are drawn as squares. F shows the image full screen, with the adjustments coming in from the right edge. See [Library](Library#looking-closely). The image surround grey is set in Settings. The filmstrip along the bottom opens another image with one click; see [Library](Library#filmstrip).

On an HDR display, highlights above paper white are shown using the display's headroom, up to two stops, with the HDR display switch to preview the SDR result. The image follows the screen's brightness without re-rendering, and the screen's extended range (which raises the backlight) is only switched on while the image needs it.

At the top of the right panel, a switch picks the scope: **Histogram**, **Waveform** (each channel's brightness against position across the image) or **Vectorscope** (colour, with neutral in the centre). Only the one shown is computed. The histogram draws red, green and blue with a luminance outline. Below it, the share of pixels clipped in the highlights and in the shadows per channel, and, with HDR display on, the share of the image above SDR white, which an SDR screen or file can't show. While a slider is dragged, the scopes update up to ten times a second.
